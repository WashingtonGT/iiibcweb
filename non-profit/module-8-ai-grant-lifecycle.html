Why This Module Exists

Most non-profits do not run "departments" — they run projects, each with a start, an end, a funder, a budget, and a reporting calendar. That structure is the single biggest obstacle to sustaining AI. A tool adopted inside one grant disappears when the grant closes. The one person who learned it leaves. The data created never reaches the next proposal. The result is a sector that re-learns the same lessons every funding cycle — exactly the gap Assessment Section 8 measures.

At the same time, the project lifecycle is where AI's most concrete, low-risk wins live. Grant reporting is the clearest case. Section 5.6 of the assessment flags reporting that consumes more than 20% of a full-time role across active grants as a "critical burden / strong AI case." Synthesizing activity logs, outcome data, and financial summaries into a funder's format is repetitive, structured, deadline-driven work — and AI is good at exactly that, when a human reviews and the use is disclosed.

But the lifecycle is also where AI can quietly cause real harm to your organization. In 2025, funders moved from informal guidance to enforceable policy. NIH's NOT-OD-25-132 (issued July 17, 2025; effective September 25, 2025) refuses applications "substantially developed by AI," caps each PI at six submissions per year, and routes post-award violations to the Office of Research Integrity (directional; NIH-specific). Horizon Europe now requires explicit disclosure of AI tool use. And AI fabricates: a 2025 study found hallucinated-citation rates ranging from roughly 14% to 95% across 13 large language models tested (directional). A fabricated citation in a funder document is not a typo — it is a research-integrity finding.

This module threads that needle. It maps where AI helps at each stage, builds the human-review and disclosure step into the workflow rather than bolting it on, and treats sustainability — budgeting, close-out knowledge capture, and tool sunsetting — as a first-class design problem, not an afterthought.

Signal

The goal is not "use AI on this grant." It is to make AI a repeatable, governed, disclosed practice that survives the grant — embedded in your templates so the next project director inherits it instead of reinventing it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, participants will be able to:

  1. Map where AI helps — and where it must stay human — across all five grant-lifecycle stages (proposal, implementation, M&E, reporting, close-out) using a grant-lifecycle AI map.
  2. Build a credible technology / AI line into a project and grant-budget template, with cost basis and sustainability plan, defensible to a funder.
  3. Run an AI-assisted grant report workflow that synthesizes activity logs, outcome data, and financial summaries into a funder's format — with a defined human-review checkpoint and a disclosure decision.
  4. State the current funder landscape on AI disclosure and originality, and apply the rule: never let AI generate the reasoning or fabricate a citation; disclose use you cannot defend without disclosing.
  5. Structure an M&E / MEL framework so it generates AI-ready outcome data (consistent indicators, structured fields, linked "why" text) and use AI to detect patterns and stress-test a theory of change — without overclaiming attribution.
  6. Complete a project close-out AI knowledge-capture template so tools, prompts, data, and lessons survive turnover and the next funding cycle.
  7. Decide how each AI tool will be transitioned, maintained, or sunset at project end, so the organization is never left with an unsupported dependency.

Core Content

Part 1 — The grant lifecycle as an AI map (with the human gate at every stage)

A grant moves through five stages. AI can help at each — but the kind of help, and the risk, changes stage to stage. The non-negotiable design rule, drawn from the 4D Diligence habit: every AI output that touches a funder, a beneficiary, or a compliance record passes through a human-review and disclosure gate before it leaves your hands.

Safeguard

Two facts must never originate from AI in a grant document: (1) a number — every metric comes from your own activity logs, M&E system, or finance records; and (2) a citation — AI fabricates references that look perfect. AI may arrange and narrate your verified numbers and real citations. It may not produce them.

Non-profit example. A youth-employment nonprofit runs a 3-year, foundation-funded program. AI drafts the quarterly report's narrative from the M&E team's verified placement numbers, themes the 240 open-text exit-survey comments into five recurring themes, and reformats it all into the funder's template. The program director verifies every number against the database, reads the themed quotes against a sample of raw comments, removes one AI-inferred causal claim ("the program caused a 30% wage increase" → "participants reported a median wage increase of 30%; see limitations"), and adds the org's standard AI-use disclosure line. Time on the report drops from two days to half a day. Nothing false reaches the funder.

Part 2 — The AI / technology budget line and sustainability plan

The reason AI dies at grant close-out is usually that it was never funded as a line item with a plan to outlive the grant. Fix this at the proposal stage. (This connects directly to Module 5's mission-ROI case and to Assessment 8.1 and 8.7.)

What goes in the technology / AI budget line:

PROJECT BUDGET — TECHNOLOGY / AI LINE (illustrative structure)
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────┐
│ Item                                           │ Basis                    │
├───────────────────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────┤
│ Managed AI tool seats (no-training-on-data)    │ # staff × monthly × mos. │
│ M&E / data platform or survey tool             │ annual license, prorated │
│ Staff time: setup, prompt-library build        │ hours × loaded rate      │
│ Staff time: AI fluency training (Module 4)     │ hours × loaded rate      │
│ Allocated to indirect/admin where appropriate  │ per your NICRA/de minimis│
└───────────────────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┘
Practitioner note

Many small non-profits with no negotiated indirect rate can use the 10% de minimis indirect-cost rate on modified total direct costs under federal Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200.414) — a place general technology and AI overhead can live. Confirm allowability with the specific funder; foundation rules differ from federal.

The sustainability question funders actually ask: "Who will manage this technology, and how will it be sustained after the grant period?" You must answer it in the proposal. There are three honest answers, and naming one is required:

  1. Absorb — the tool becomes part of core operations, funded from unrestricted/general operating after grant end.
  2. Re-fund — the tool is rebuilt into the next grant's budget (state the plan; do not assume).
  3. Sunset — the tool was project-specific and will be retired at close-out (then say so, and plan the data export — see Part 5).
Signal

"We will figure it out later" is what creates unsupported dependencies. A funder respects "this tool sunsets at close-out and we export the data" far more than silence that becomes a stranded cost. Decide the path in the proposal.

Part 4 — Funder rules on AI: disclosure, originality, and the fabrication trap

The 2024–2026 funder landscape settled on a consistent ask: be honest about whether and how you used AI, keep the reasoning yours, and never fabricate. Specifics vary by funder, so the operating standard is simple — use AI in a way you can defend if a program officer asks.

Funder typeCurrent stance (directional; verify per funder)
NIH (federal)NOT-OD-25-132: refuses applications "substantially developed by AI"; AI as disclosed assistant (grammar, formatting, polishing non-native English) is acceptable, AI as undisclosed ghostwriter of scientific reasoning is not; six-application annual cap per PI; post-award violations → Office of Research Integrity.
NSF (federal)Disclosure-based framework signaled for applicants; reviewers prohibited from uploading proposal content to non-approved AI tools (confidentiality).
Horizon Europe (international)Explicit disclosure required if AI tools helped draft/develop the proposal.
Most private foundationsFew standalone AI policies yet; in practice deferring to the federal funder-of-record standard, and some program staff now flag proposals that "read like a language model wrote them."
Safeguard

Fabricated citations are the fastest way to lose a funder's trust. AI invents references that look real — correct-looking authors, journals, years — that do not exist. In a grant document this is treated as an integrity violation, not a mistake. Verify every citation against the actual source. If you cannot find the real source, the claim does not go in.

A defensible org disclosure stance (adopt and keep consistent):

  • Maintain a per-document AI-use log: which sections used AI, which tool, and what you verified.
  • Default to disclosing AI assistance to funders unless the funder's rules clearly make it unnecessary — disclosure you didn't strictly need costs nothing; undisclosed use you can't defend costs trust.
  • Keep all reasoning, claims, numbers, and citations human-owned and verified. AI assists with structure, language, and synthesis of your verified material.
Noise

Vendor claims like "AI-written proposals see a 30% higher success rate" circulate widely (directional, vendor-sourced). Treat them with skepticism — and note that several funders now penalize proposals that read as AI-generated. The win is reduced burden and better structure on your argument, not outsourcing the argument.

Part 5 — AI-ready M&E and project close-out that survives the cycle

Make your M&E generate AI-ready data. AI-assisted impact analysis is only as good as the structure of the data feeding it. Assessment 8.6 measures exactly this. The fixes are cheap and mostly about discipline at data-collection time:

DESIGNING AI-READY OUTCOME DATA
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ • Consistent indicators: same definition across cohorts, projects, time │
│ • Structured fields: dropdowns/multiselect, NOT free text, for anything │
│   you want to compare (avoids "same concept, ten spellings")            │
│ • Pre/post self-reported rating on each core outcome (comparable)       │
│ • ONE open-text "why" linked to each rating (AI themes it later)        │
│ • 1–2 demographic fields for equity analysis (with consent)             │
│ • A shared data dictionary: one definition of each metric, owned        │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

With that structure, AI can do useful M&E work: theme hundreds of open-text comments automatically, detect patterns across multi-year data, and stress-test a theory of change ("where does our outcome data support, or fail to support, each link in our logic model?"). The discipline that keeps this honest: AI surfaces patterns and poses questions; the M&E lead validates against the raw data and owns every conclusion. AI must never make a causal or attribution claim your evidence can't support.

Practitioner note

Cross-project data architecture (Assessment 8.4) is powerful but consent-gated. Data collected in one project may inform future programming only if beneficiaries consented to that use. Re-using beneficiary data across grants without consent is a Module 7 violation, not an efficiency. When in doubt, the answer is no.

Project close-out: capture knowledge before it walks out the door. Federal close-out under Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200.344) requires final reports and liquidation within 120 calendar days of the period of performance — start preparing about three months out. But the funder's close-out is about their accountability; your close-out must also preserve the AI capability for the organization. This is the step that defeats the project-model problem.

WHAT CLOSE-OUT MUST CAPTURE (so the next cycle inherits it)
  Tools used ──► seats, costs, who managed them, sunset/transition decision
  Prompts ─────► the prompt library that worked (report, M&E, comms)
  Data ────────► what was created, where it lives, consent scope, exports
  Lessons ─────► what AI helped with, what failed, what to do differently
  People ──────► who knew what (the turnover insurance)

Tool sunsetting (Assessment 8.7). When a project ends, every AI/tech tool needs an explicit decision so the org isn't left paying for or depending on something orphaned:

DecisionAction at close-out
SunsetCancel subscription; export all data first; document what was lost/retained; remove access.
TransitionMove the seat/license into core operations or another active grant; reassign the owner.
Re-fundConfirm it's in the next grant's budget before relying on it; otherwise treat as sunset.
Signal

The test of whether this module worked is a year out: a new project director, on a new grant, opens the templates and finds an AI budget line, a working report workflow, a prompt library, and a close-out record from the last project — and does not start from zero. That is AI surviving the grant cycle.

Hands-On Exercises

Exercise 1 — Build and run your AI-assisted grant report (40 min)

Scenario: Use the live grant you brought. You have one quarter's verified activity data, outcome data, a financial summary, and the funder's report template.

Steps:

  1. Gather & scrub (5 min): Assemble your verified inputs. Remove every beneficiary identifier. Confirm you're in a managed tool.
  2. Draft (10 min): Prompt the AI. Use this pattern:
    Note

    "You are helping draft a grant progress report. Below are our VERIFIED figures and the funder's required section headings. Map our data into these sections and draft the narrative. Do not invent any numbers, statistics, or citations — use only what I provide. Flag anywhere you'd need data I haven't given you. [paste verified data] [paste funder template headings]"

  3. Review at the human gate (15 min): Run the checklist below. Trace each number to its source. Find and fix any overstated/causal claim. Confirm zero fabricated citations.
  4. Disclose & sign (10 min): Write your org's one-line AI-use disclosure and your per-document log entry.

Facilitator answer key — what a good output shows:

  • Every number in the draft matches a source the participant can point to; no orphan figures.
  • At least one AI-inserted causal/overstated phrase was caught and softened (this almost always appears — use it to teach Discernment).
  • Qualitative themes were checked against a sample of raw comments.
  • A disclosure line and a log entry exist. Common miss: participants disclose nothing because "we only used it a little" — coach toward the defensible-default stance.

Exercise 2 — AI-ready M&E and a theory-of-change stress test (40 min)

Scenario: Take your project's logic model / theory of change and a slice of your outcome data (anonymized).

Steps:

  1. Audit data structure (10 min): Score your current M&E against the "AI-ready outcome data" box. Where are you using free text for things you want to compare? Where are indicator definitions inconsistent? List two fixes.
  2. Theme open-text (10 min): Paste a set of anonymized open-text responses; ask AI to identify recurring themes and pull a representative quote for each.
  3. Stress-test the theory of change (15 min): Ask: "Here is one link in our logic model: [link]. Here is the outcome data relevant to it: [data]. Where does the data support this link, and where is it weak or silent? Pose three questions our M&E should answer next. Do not assert causation."
  4. Validate (5 min): Check the themes against a sample of raw responses; mark any AI claim that overstated the evidence.

Facilitator answer key:

  • Strong answers identify gaps ("we have output data but no outcome data for this link") rather than accepting AI's confident narrative.
  • At least one over-attribution by the AI is caught ("data shows the program works" → "data is consistent with, but does not prove, the link").
  • Two concrete, cheap data-structure fixes are named (e.g., convert a free-text field to a dropdown; write one shared definition for a contested metric).

Exercise 3 — Project close-out AI knowledge-capture (25 min)

Scenario: Imagine your live grant closes in 90 days. Complete the close-out capture template (in Templates section) for it now.

Steps:

  1. List every AI/tech tool the project used, its cost, and its owner (10 min).
  2. For each tool, choose sunset / transition / re-fund and write the one action that decision requires (5 min).
  3. Paste your two or three best working prompts into the prompt-library section (5 min).
  4. Write three lessons: one thing AI helped with, one thing it failed at, one change for next time (5 min).

Facilitator answer key: A complete template names a person against each tool and a data export step against every "sunset." The most common gap is a tool with no named owner — flag it: "unowned tool = stranded cost or lost capability at close-out."

Knowledge Check

  1. (MCQ) In an AI-assisted grant report, where must the numbers come from? a) The AI tool's best estimate · b) The funder's prior report · c) Your own verified systems (activity logs, M&E, finance) · d) A web search
  2. (MCQ) A funder asks whether you used AI on a proposal. The safest default org stance is to: a) Deny it to avoid suspicion · b) Disclose AI assistance and keep a per-document log · c) Only disclose if directly required in writing · d) Let the AI write the disclosure
  3. (Short answer) Name the two things that must never originate from AI in a grant document, and why.
  4. (Short answer) Your project ends in 90 days and used a paid AI tool funded only by that grant. List the three sustainability options and what each requires at close-out.
  5. (MCQ) Which M&E design choice best produces AI-ready outcome data? a) Free-text fields for everything · b) Dropdowns/multiselect for anything you want to compare, plus one linked open-text "why" · c) Collecting more demographic data without consent · d) Letting AI define the indicators
  6. (Short answer) Why is re-using beneficiary data collected in one project to inform a future project not automatically allowed?
  7. (MCQ) AI proposes a clear causal claim from your M&E data ("the program caused a 30% gain"). The M&E lead should: a) Use it — it sounds strong · b) Validate against raw data and soften to a defensible, non-causal statement · c) Ask AI to find a citation supporting it · d) Submit and add a disclaimer later
  8. (Short answer) What is the difference between the funder's close-out and your organization's close-out, and why does the org's matter for AI?

Answer key

  1. c. Numbers come from your own verified systems; AI invents plausible figures if asked to produce them.
  2. b. Disclose-by-default plus a per-document log is defensible; denial and after-the-fact disclosure are not.
  3. Numbers and citations. AI fabricates both convincingly; in a funder document a fabricated citation is treated as an integrity violation, and an invented number is a false statement. AI may arrange/narrate verified numbers and real citations only.
  4. Absorb (fund from general operating after grant end), Re-fund (build into next grant budget — confirm, don't assume), Sunset (retire at close-out; export all data first). Each needs a named owner.
  5. b. Structured fields enable comparison; one linked "why" gives AI text to theme; equity fields require consent.
  6. Because consent is use-specific — beneficiaries consented to the original project's use, not a future one. Cross-project reuse without consent is a beneficiary-safeguard violation (Module 7), not an efficiency.
  7. b. AI surfaces patterns; humans validate against raw data and never overstate attribution.
  8. The funder's close-out (e.g., 2 CFR 200.344's 120-day reports/liquidation) serves the funder's accountability. The org's close-out captures tools, prompts, data, lessons, and people so AI capability survives turnover and the next cycle — defeating the project-model problem that kills AI adoption.

Facilitator Guide

Prep checklist (free / low-cost):

  • Confirm every participant brings a real, active grant with its reporting template and recent data. Without this the workshop falls flat.
  • Have one managed AI tool available (Claude.ai, Microsoft Copilot, or ChatGPT Team — no-training-on-data). Confirm it's on the org's approved-tool catalog (Module 3).
  • Pre-anonymize a sample outcome dataset and a sample of open-text comments as a fallback for anyone whose data isn't ready.
  • Print Templates A–E.
  • Have the org's AI policy and do-not-use list (Module 7) and cardinal data rule (Module 2) on hand.

Timing: The two hands-on exercises (1 and 2) are the heart; protect their time. If running long, compress Part 5 lecture and push Exercise 3 to a follow-up — but never cut the human-review gate teaching in Exercise 1.

Common pitfalls:

  • Pasting real PII into the tool mid-exercise. Stop immediately; reinforce the cardinal rule. Use this as a teachable moment, not a gotcha.
  • Accepting AI numbers. Watch for participants who let AI "fill in" a missing figure. This is the highest-risk habit; catch it live.
  • Skipping disclosure as "too small to matter." Coach the defensible-default stance.
  • Treating sustainability as optional. If a team can't name who manages a tool after grant end, they've found their gap.

Discussion prompts:

  • "Which of our active grants has the heaviest reporting burden? What would half that time buy us in program delivery?"
  • "Name one tool we adopted in a past grant that we lost when the grant closed. What did that cost us?"
  • "Where in our M&E are we collecting data in a way AI can't use — and what's the cheapest fix?"

Tailoring by audience:

  • Program Directors (A3): emphasize the lifecycle map, the human gate, and overstated-claim risk.
  • M&E / MEL staff: weight Part 5 and Exercise 2 (AI-ready data, theory-of-change stress test).
  • Grants / Operations / Finance (A5): weight Part 2 (budget line, indirect costs, sustainability) and the financial-summary inputs to reporting.

Addressing fear/resistance: The honest fear here is "AI will fabricate something and our funder relationship will pay for it." Validate it — that fear is correct, and it's exactly why the human-review and disclosure gate exists. Frame AI as cutting the reporting drudgery so staff get hours back for the mission, while the gate keeps the organization safe. Celebrate the first report that ships in half the time with zero errors caught downstream.
