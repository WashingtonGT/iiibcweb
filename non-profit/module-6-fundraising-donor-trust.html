Donor trust is the one asset you cannot rebuild with a tool, even though AI can help you understand donors and write to them faster than ever. Why this module exists

Donor trust is a non-profit's most valuable and least replaceable asset, and AI now touches it every day. AI sits in the middle of almost every fundraising task: scoring who is about to lapse, drafting the appeal, personalizing the ask, and summarizing the major-donor file before a meeting. Done well, this frees fundraisers to spend more time with people and less time at a keyboard. Done carelessly, it quietly swaps an algorithm for the human relationship donors are actually paying for. One fabricated detail or one mishandled data record can cost trust that took years to build.

Donors have noticed, and their data is what worries them most. In the Fundraising.AI Donor Perceptions of AI 2025 survey (~1,031 U.S. donors, August 2025), data stewardship was donors' top concern. Roughly two-thirds feared privacy breaches or insecure data handling, and a majority (~55 percent) worried AI could bake in bias (directional). Donors also expect disclosure: according to AFP's sector reporting, a large majority say it matters that non-profits plainly disclose where and why AI is used, how humans stay in control, and what evidence shows it works (directional). Yet adoption data from the same period shows nearly 80 percent of non-profits using AI in some form, while only ~9 percent feel ready to use it responsibly and fewer than ~7 percent have an internal AI policy (directional). This module closes that gap for the development team.

Fundraisers already use AI. The real risks are over-automation, which hands empathy to a model, and under-governance, which means pasting a donor's name, capacity rating, or giving history into a public tool that may train on it. This module gives the team a disciplined way to get AI's speed without trading away the authenticity and data stewardship donors are now explicitly watching for.

Signal

The fundraising win from AI is more fundraiser time with humans, because AI absorbed the drafting and research grunt-work. "More emails per hour" misses the point. Every output still passes through human Discernment and Diligence before it reaches a donor.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, participants will be able to:

  1. Use AI for donor segmentation, lapsed-donor / churn signals, and prospect insight, and state plainly what these scores can and cannot tell you.
  2. Apply donor-data privacy rules to AI use: distinguish what is safe to put into a tool (segment descriptions, anonymized trends, draft copy) from what is never pasted into a public tool (donor PII, meaning personally identifiable information, plus capacity ratings and giving history).
  3. Draft appeals, thank-yous, newsletters, and grant narratives with a reusable prompt-pattern library in which every pattern has a built-in human-review step.
  4. Apply Discernment + Diligence before anything reaches a donor: catch hallucinated facts, fabricated donor details, off-voice tone, and bias.
  5. Decide and apply an AI-content disclosure stance using a decision guide, and articulate why for any given piece.
  6. Recognize and avoid over-automation that makes donor relationships feel transactional, using a human-touch rule of thumb.
  7. Prepare leadership and board talking points for donor and public questions about how the organization uses AI.
  8. Map the team's practice to a recognized responsible-fundraising-AI framework (Fundraising.AI's pillars; AFP ethics).

Core content

Part 1: What AI actually does in fundraising (and what it can't)

AI does two jobs in fundraising, and they fail differently, so they need different safeguards. The first is analysis: pattern-finding over your donor data, usually inside your CRM or a connected tool. The second is generation: drafting text.

FUNDRAISING AI — TWO JOBS, TWO RISK PROFILES
┌──────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ ANALYSIS (scoring/predicting) │ GENERATION (drafting text)                    │
│  • donor segmentation         │  • appeal / email drafts                      │
│  • lapsed / churn risk score  │  • thank-you / acknowledgment                 │
│  • giving-propensity score    │  • newsletters, social, case for support      │
│  • prospect / wealth insight  │  • grant narratives, gift proposals           │
│  • optimized ask amount       │                                               │
│ MAIN RISK: biased / opaque    │ MAIN RISK: hallucinated facts, fabricated     │
│ scores; over-trusting a number│ donor details, off-voice / transactional tone │
└──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Analysis use cases, in plain terms. Segmentation groups donors by behavior so you stop sending everyone the same letter. Churn / lapse prediction flags regular givers who look likely to stop, early enough to intervene. One published vendor case (Greenpeace via Dataro) reported retaining 64 at-risk recurring donors, an estimated ~$23k saved (directional, single-org). Propensity / wealth screening estimates likelihood and capacity to give, so a major-gifts officer can prioritize limited hours. Optimized ask amounts suggest a gift ask based on past behavior. Tools here include Blackbaud predictive analytics / ResearchPoint, Salesforce / Agentforce Nonprofit (prospect research, gift-proposal drafting, donor summaries; early-adopter claims of 4–6 hrs/week saved are directional), Virtuous Insights, DonorSearch Ai, and Dataro.

What a score is, and what it isn't. A propensity or churn score is a probability based on past patterns, not a verdict about a person. It can be wrong, it can be biased (capacity models can track wealth, race, and zip code together), and it is only as good as the messy donor data underneath it. Use it to prioritize attention, never to write someone off or to make an automated decision about a human.

Safeguard

A wealth/capacity rating is sensitive personal data and can feel invasive to the donor it describes. Keep capacity ratings, screening results, and giving history inside your secure CRM: they are exactly the kind of data Part 2 says never to paste into a public AI tool.

DoDon't
Use scores to decide who to call first and what to testTreat a score as a fact about a donor's worth or intentions
Ask the vendor how a model was built and what data it usesAssume the model is neutral because it is "just math"
Re-check segments against what your team knows about real peopleLet a segment label override a relationship you actually have

Part 2: The donor-data privacy line (the rule that protects trust)

The cardinal rule from Module 4 applies with full force to donor data, which makes this the most important page in the module: never paste donor personal information into a public/consumer AI tool that may train on your inputs. Donor names, contact details, giving history, and capacity/wealth ratings are all sensitive. So are health or family circumstances mentioned in moves-management notes (records of each step in cultivating a donor). Much of this data is regulated: 20+ U.S. states now have consumer-privacy laws, and PCI DSS 4.0, the payment-card security standard, applies to card processing (directional).

Fundraisers then ask, "How do I use AI at all?" The answer is the safe/unsafe split:

DONOR-DATA + AI — THE SPLIT
SAFE to put in an AI tool          NEVER in a public AI tool
──────────────────────────────     ──────────────────────────────────────
• De-identified segment            • Donor names, emails, addresses, phone
  DESCRIPTIONS ("lapsed monthly    • Specific giving history / amounts tied
  donors who gave 2–3 yrs")          to a named person
• High-level, aggregate trends     • Wealth / capacity / propensity ratings
• Your own draft copy to improve   • Moves-management / relationship notes
• Public info you'd publish        • Anything health, family, immigration,
  anyway (mission, program facts)    or hardship-related about a person

Two tiers of tool change what is allowed. Enterprise / managed AI (your CRM's built-in AI operating on your own data under contract, or a paid business tier that contractually does not train on your inputs) can work with real donor records because the data stays governed. Free consumer tools generally may store or learn from what you type, so they get de-identified inputs only. When in doubt, de-identify, or use the in-CRM feature instead of copying data out.

Signal

AI can hallucinate (invent plausible but false) details about a donor, and that is a real reputational risk. A model asked to "personalize" a thank-you can invent a gift amount, a program, or a family detail. The fix is Part 4: a human who knows the donor verifies every personalized fact before send.

Part 3: The fundraising prompt-pattern library (with the human-review step built in)

Build a small shared library of prompt patterns so good prompting survives turnover and everyone drafts to the same standard. A prompt pattern is a reusable template: you keep the structure and swap in the specifics. Every pattern in the library ends with the same non-negotiable line: a human-review step before anything reaches a donor. (The full patterns are under Templates and takeaway artifacts.)

THE 4D LOOP ON A FUNDRAISING DRAFT
 DELEGATION   → Is this safe to hand to AI? (no donor PII in a public tool;
                no automated decision about a person)
 DESCRIPTION  → Give it role, audience, voice, length, must-include facts,
                and what NOT to say
 DISCERNMENT  → Check every fact/number/name; check the voice; check for bias
 DILIGENCE    → Edit to the org's voice, verify, decide disclosure, OWN the send

Good Description for fundraising always includes: who the donor is (segment, not PII), the organization's voice (warm, specific, never hype), the must-include facts (a real program outcome you can verify), length, and a "do not invent" instruction ("use only the facts I give you; do not add statistics, donor details, or quotes"). That last instruction is the single highest-value habit for reducing hallucinations in donor copy.

Practitioner note

Paste a paragraph of your actual best appeal into the tool as a voice sample and say "match this voice." It is the fastest way to stop AI drafts from sounding like a generic non-profit robot. Donors and even funder reviewers now read a generic, over-polished tone as "AI wrote this."

Part 4: Discernment + Diligence before a donor sees it

The review pass before sending is where you protect trust. AI generation has three failure modes that are especially dangerous in donor communication: hallucinated facts (invented stats, fake research, wrong program names), fabricated donor details (a personalized line that is simply not true of this donor), and off-voice / transactional tone (technically fine but emotionally hollow, which makes donors feel processed by a machine).

Run a quick, repeatable review pass on every AI-assisted donor piece:

THE "BEFORE A DONOR SEES IT" CHECK  (≤ 5 min)
[ ] Every fact, number, name, date, and quote VERIFIED against a real source
[ ] No fabricated donor detail — personalized claims checked by someone who
    knows the donor or the record
[ ] Reads in OUR voice, to a real person — not generic, not over-flattering
[ ] No bias / no language that could alienate the communities we serve
[ ] Disclosure decision made (see Part 5)
[ ] A named human owns this send (Diligence — your name is on it, not the AI's)
Safeguard

Treat AI as a fast, capable, occasionally-wrong intern who has never met your donors. You would never let an intern send a major-donor thank-you unread. Same rule.

Part 5: The AI-content disclosure decision

Be open about your practice in proportion to the piece; you don't need a label on every email. The sector answer in 2025–2026 is converging on transparency about practice, proportionate to the piece. AFP's ethics direction and the Fundraising.AI framework both point the same way: donors should be able to learn where and why you use AI and how humans stay in control. Many donors will reward openness, and a meaningful minority say undisclosed AI use would make them less likely to give (directional). An honest, discoverable stance matters more than any footnote.

A workable stance for a lean shop:

  • Always disclosable on request, and stated once publicly (e.g., a short line in your privacy/AI-use statement: "We sometimes use AI tools to help draft communications and analyze giving patterns; a staff member reviews and is accountable for everything we send, and we never share donor personal data with public AI tools").
  • No per-message label needed for AI-assisted drafting that a human substantially edited and owns. This is like using spell-check or a template.
  • Stronger disclosure when AI did something a donor would want to know about: an automated reply from an AI agent (software that acts without a person) with no human in the loop, AI-driven personalization of content (not just formatting), or AI used in something the donor would reasonably expect to be personal (a condolence note, a major-donor letter). The decision guide in the artifacts section walks through this case by case.
Signal

Trust breaks when "we used AI, hid it, and a donor found out." Using AI alone rarely does the damage. Decide your stance, write it down, and make it easy to find. Disclosure done well is a differentiator donors reward.

Part 6: Over-automation, authenticity, and board/donor talking points

The deepest risk is cultural: automating so much that donors feel like records in a machine. A useful rule of thumb in the sector is 10-80-10. A human sets strategy for the first 10 percent, AI drafts the middle 80 percent, and a human does the final 10 percent (verify, personalize, own). The human bookends are non-negotiable for anything relational. AI should buy fundraisers more time for calls, visits, and handwritten notes, never replace them.

Board / leadership talking points. Development leadership should prepare the board and ED to answer donor and public questions calmly and consistently:

If a donor / reporter asks…Say (and be able to show)
"Do you use AI on my donations / my data?""We use AI to help draft communications and spot giving patterns. We never put donor personal data into public AI tools, and a named staff member reviews and is accountable for everything you receive."
"Was this letter written by a robot?""A person on our team wrote and approved that letter. AI may have helped with a first draft, the same way we use templates and editors—but the words and the accountability are ours."
"Isn't this a waste of donor money?""It's the opposite: it frees our team from hours of admin so they spend more time with supporters and beneficiaries. We track that time saved."
"How do I know it's accurate / unbiased?""Every fact and number is human-verified before it goes out, and we review our donor models for bias. We can share our AI-use principles."
"Can I opt out of AI handling my data?""Yes—tell us and we'll note it. Here's our AI-use and privacy statement."
Practitioner note

Map your practice to a named framework so the board sees rigor, not improvisation. The ten pillars of the Fundraising.AI framework make a ready-made checklist: privacy and security, data ethics, inclusiveness and bias mitigation, accountability/transparency/explainability, continuous learning, collaboration, legal compliance, social impact, sustainability, and mission alignment. AFP's Code of Ethical Standards is enforceable for members.

Hands-on exercises

Exercise 1: Build and red-team a lapsed-donor appeal (45 min)

Scenario. Your monthly-giving program has a cluster of donors who gave for 2–3 years and stopped ~8 months ago. You want a re-engagement email. You have a de-identified segment description and three verifiable program facts.

Steps:

  1. Delegation check: Confirm you are using only the segment description (no names, no amounts tied to individuals) and an approved tool. (2 min)
  2. Description: Use the appeal draft pattern (artifacts). Include a voice sample, the three real facts, length, and the "do not invent" instruction. Generate a draft. (10 min)
  3. Discernment red-team: Swap drafts with a partner. Hunt for: any invented statistic, any program/fact you cannot verify, any fabricated donor detail, generic/over-flattering tone, anything that could alienate the communities you serve. Mark every one. (12 min)
  4. Diligence: Rewrite to your voice, verify the three facts against a real source, and decide disclosure (Part 5). (15 min)
  5. Debrief: How many hallucinations/fabrications did the room find collectively? (6 min)

Facilitator answer key (what teams typically catch):

  • A fabricated stat the model added on its own ("studies show monthly donors are 5x more loyal")—delete; it was invented.
  • A made-up donor-specific line ("Since your first gift in 2019…")—unverifiable per-donor in a segment send; cut or templatize safely.
  • Over-flattery / guilt-tripping that is off-voice—rewrite warm and specific.
  • Tone that treats lapsing as the donor's failure—bias toward respect; reframe.
  • Teaching point: the "do not invent" instruction in step 2 should have prevented most fabrications; teams that skipped it find more. That is the lesson.

Exercise 2: Disclosure decision sprint (20 min)

Run each case through the disclosure decision guide (artifacts) and decide: no label needed / disclose / strengthen disclosure or add human step.

  1. AI helped draft a year-end appeal; a fundraiser heavily edited and signed it. → No per-message label; covered by public AI-use statement.
  2. An AI agent auto-replies to donor email inquiries with no human review. → Disclose clearly that they're interacting with an automated assistant; add human escalation.
  3. AI generated a "personalized" major-donor proposal grounded in CRM data; an officer reviewed it. → No label, but officer must verify every personalized fact (Part 4); high-relationship piece.
  4. A condolence note to a bereaved donor, AI-drafted. → Strengthen the human step—this should be substantially human-written; authenticity matters most here.
  5. Newsletter copy lightly AI-polished. → No label; like spell-check.

Facilitator note: the throughline is human-in-the-loop + proportionality. Disclose more as (a) human involvement drops and (b) the donor's expectation of personal touch rises.

Exercise 3: Draft the board talking-points one-pager (15 min)

In pairs, fill the talking-points table (Part 6) with your organization's real specifics: your actual tools, your actual data rule, one true time-saved example. Output is a one-pager the ED and board chair keep on hand.

Facilitator guide

Prep checklist:

  • Confirm the org's approved tool tier and whether anyone may use real donor data in it (Module 3). If the only tool is consumer-grade, prepare fully fictional/anonymized donor examples for every exercise.
  • Pull a de-identified real segment, one real appeal (for the voice sample), and three verifiable program facts.
  • Print Artifacts C (do/don't card) and B (disclosure guide) for every participant.
  • Have the org's current donor-privacy statement on hand; if none exists, this session can draft the one-liner in Artifact B.

Free / low-cost materials: Fundraising.AI framework page; AFP Code of Ethical Standards; Anthropic + GivingTuesday AI Fluency for Nonprofits; your existing CRM's AI documentation (Blackbaud, Salesforce/Agentforce, Bloomerang, Virtuous, DonorSearch, Dataro).

Timing: Protect Part 3 (library build) and Part 4 (red-team), because they carry the behavior change. If short on time, cut the Part 1 lecture, not the hands-on work.

Common pitfalls:

  • Tool runs ahead of governance. Fundraisers already paste donor data into ChatGPT. Name it without blame; redirect to the safe/unsafe split and in-CRM options.
  • Disclosure paralysis. Teams fixate on labeling every email. Anchor on the decision guide: proportionate, human-in-the-loop, public statement.
  • AI-voice creep. Drafts drift generic. Enforce the voice-sample habit; read drafts aloud.
  • Treating scores as truth. Repeat: a score prioritizes attention; it never decides about a person.

Discussion prompts: "What do our biggest donors actually feel about AI? Do we know, or are we guessing?" (ties to assessment 6.5) · "Where are we already over-automated and it shows?" · "What would we want disclosed if we were the donor?"

Tailor by audience: A4 gets the full workshop. A1/board get Parts 5–6 and Artifact D. A5 ops who handle gift data get Part 2 and Artifact C.

Addressing fear and resistance: Expect two fears: that AI makes the fundraising craft fake, and that staff will break a data rule. Answer both. AI does the drafting grunt-work so fundraisers do more of the relationship work they came into the field for. The do/don't card makes the data rule simple enough that nobody has to guess. Celebrate the first "AI saved me an afternoon, so I made three donor calls instead" story publicly.
