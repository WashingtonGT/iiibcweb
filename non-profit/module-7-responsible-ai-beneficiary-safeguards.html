Why this module exists

This is the one module where "move fast and learn" is the wrong instinct. Most AI mistakes in a non-profit are recoverable: you can re-send a clumsy donor email or correct a wrong number in a grant report. But the people your programs serve (people in poverty, in trauma, displaced, navigating immigration, or in mental-health crisis) often live one bad decision away from losing housing, safety, custody, a benefit, or a legal case. When AI touches them, the stakes change completely.

The harms are documented. Michigan's MiDAS system falsely accused roughly 40,000 residents of unemployment fraud after the state removed human review, and a later audit found that at least 93 percent of the automated fraud accusations were wrong (IEEE Spectrum, 2024; U-M Ford School, 2024). The Allegheny County family-screening tool helped caseworkers decide which families to investigate. It drew U.S. Department of Justice civil-rights scrutiny over whether it hardened bias against people with disabilities and mental-health conditions (PBS NewsHour / AP, 2023; ACLU, 2024). Researchers studying benefits systems in multiple states conclude that fraud-detection algorithms aimed at the poor frequently punish the poor instead (The Conversation, 2024; Eubanks, Automating Inequality). These were public agencies with budgets and lawyers. A lean non-profit pasting a case note into a free chatbot has none of those guardrails.

Your program and service-delivery staff should leave this module knowing exactly where the lines are, why they exist, and how to hold them even when AI would be faster. We structure the ethics content on the three dimensions of the TAG + Project Evident Responsible AI Adoption in Philanthropy framework (Organizational, Ethical, Technical), which more than 300 grantmakers helped develop (TAG, 2023; updated guidance 2024–2026).

Note

Research note (directional): Usage is racing ahead of safeguards. Only about 15 percent of non-profits have an organizational responsible-AI-use policy (Candid AI Equity Project, 2025), and equity-practice implementation dropped to ~36 percent in 2025 from ~46 percent in 2024. Treat the percentages as directional; the gap is real.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, participants will be able to:

  1. Apply the organization's "do-not-use" list by naming the high-stakes categories where AI is off-limits: eligibility/benefit decisions, crisis/safety communications, case notes with sensitive data, mental-health support, and HR/performance.
  2. Treat AI as a collaborator, not an authority, and explain in plain words why "the computer said so" is never an acceptable basis for a decision about a person.
  3. Recognize AI bias against served populations (racial, language, socioeconomic, disability), and run a bias check before deployment and on a regular schedule afterward.
  4. Obtain trauma-informed, culturally appropriate informed consent before AI processes beneficiary data, using a consent guide instead of a buried checkbox.
  5. Apply heightened human-in-the-loop control on any crisis- or safety-critical decision, and never route a person in crisis to an unsupervised AI.
  6. Protect sensitive data: never enter immigration status, health, abuse, or financial-distress details into public AI tools, and connect this rule to GDPR and HIPAA duties in plain terms.
  7. Check AI tools for accessibility so they do not create new barriers for beneficiaries with disabilities, limited literacy, language differences, or limited technology access.
  8. Use the beneficiary-concern escalation path so the people you serve have a real way to question how technology is used in their care.
  9. Map each safeguard to the Organizational, Ethical, or Technical dimension so the rules connect to the organization's policy, not just to one workshop.

Core content

Part 1: The map: Organizational, Ethical, Technical

Responsible AI rests on three layers of responsibility, and all three have to agree. The TAG + Project Evident framework names them Organizational, Ethical, Technical, and we use those three buckets all day so every safeguard has a home (TAG + Project Evident, 2023–2026).

DimensionThe question it answersWhat it owns in a non-profit
OrganizationalAre we set up to be accountable?Who is responsible · the do-not-use list · policy approved by leadership/board · how staff escalate · how beneficiaries raise concerns · staff (and beneficiary) involvement in the rules
EthicalDoes this respect the people we serve?Consent · fairness / bias · transparency with beneficiaries · dignity · cultural fit · "human flourishing, minimize risk, maximize benefit"
TechnicalIs the tool itself safe and fit?What data the tool can touch · bias testing · accessibility · human-in-the-loop controls · security · monitoring after deployment
 
        ORGANIZATIONAL  ── who is accountable, what is allowed
              │
              ▼
        ETHICAL  ── consent · fairness · dignity · transparency
              │
              ▼
        TECHNICAL  ── data limits · bias test · accessibility · human-in-loop
              │
              ▼
   A decision that protects, never harms, the person served
Signal

A program staffer who can say which dimension a worry lives in—"that's an Ethical problem: we never got consent"—because naming it maps straight to a fix and an owner.

Noise

A glossy vendor claim that a tool is "responsible AI" or "bias-free." No tool is bias-free. Responsibility lives in your practices across all three dimensions, not in a vendor badge.

Safeguard

If a proposed AI use fails any one of the three dimensions, it does not proceed, even if the other two are strong. A technically excellent tool used without consent is still off-limits.

Say the framework's own north star out loud to the room: AI adoption should "enable human flourishing, minimize risk, and maximize benefit". More than 300 grantmakers helped build it as a living document, not a finished rulebook (TAG + Project Evident, 2023). Use that phrase as a gut-check. Before any AI touches a person you serve, ask the three-dimension questions in order: Are we accountable for this? Does it respect this person? Is the tool itself safe and fit? If you cannot answer all three with a confident yes, you have found your stop.

Practitioner note

These three dimensions are practical. In a lean non-profit, the same person often wears all three hats in one afternoon: drafting the policy (Organizational), having the consent conversation (Ethical), and clicking "run" on the tool (Technical). Naming the dimensions helps that person notice when they have skipped a hat.

4D tie-in—Delegation: The whole module is one extended act of Delegation: deciding, deliberately, what you hand to AI and what stays human. In program work the default flips. Keep it human unless you can prove it is safe to delegate.

Part 2: The do-not-use list: What AI must never decide

The do-not-use list is the most important single artifact in the kit. Candid calls the "When NOT to use AI" section "the most important section" of any non-profit AI policy (Candid, 2025). Its principle comes straight from the 4D framework and Vera Solutions' Human Oversight & Control principle: treat AI as a collaborator, not an authority. Ask it for sources, check its facts, and never let "the model said so" stand in for a human judgment about a person (Vera Solutions, 2024; Candid, 2025).

Start with this do-not-use list. Adopt it, then add your own program-specific rows in Exercise 1.

AI must NOT be used to…WhyReal-world warning
Decide eligibility for a benefit, service, or fundingErrors deny help to people who qualify; bias compoundsMiDAS: ~93% of automated fraud findings wrong (IEEE Spectrum, 2024)
Write or auto-send communications during crisis, grief, domestic violence, or traumaTone-deaf or wrong output can re-traumatize or endangerCandid do-not-use list (2025)
Create case notes containing sensitive personal data (health, immigration, abuse, finances)Public tools may retain and train on the text; leak riskModule 2 cardinal rule
Provide mental-health support / act as a crisis counselorChatbots have impersonated 988 and given wrong/nonexistent crisis numbersCrisisTalk, 2025; Futurism, 2025
Conduct performance reviews or monitor employeesHigh-stakes HR decisions need human judgment and fairnessCandid do-not-use list (2025)
Generate content about a community without their consent and contextMisrepresents the people you serve; erodes trustVera Ethics & Community Values (2024)
Make any decision where wrong info could cause harm and you can't confidently verify itThe catch-all for cases the rows above don't nameCandid (2025)
Safeguard

The do-not-use list is a stop list, not a "get approval first" list. These uses are off-limits regardless of deadline pressure. AI may assist around them (e.g., summarizing public eligibility rules for a staffer to apply) but never make the decision.

Practitioner note

Candid's research is blunt about one failure mode: fear-based policies that ban everything push AI use underground, where staff use unapproved tools and stop asking questions (Candid, 2025). Pair the list with a clear do-use lane (Part 5) and a no-blame way to ask. Aim for responsible, confident use, not silence.

Do / Don't

  • Do let AI draft the neutral, public-information parts of a workflow (a benefits-program fact sheet, a generic appointment reminder) that a human then reviews.
  • Do keep the do-not-use list posted where caseworkers actually work, and re-read it at onboarding.
  • Don't paste a real case note, intake form, or anything with a beneficiary's identity into a public chatbot to "just clean it up."
  • Don't let an AI score, rank, or triage real people without a named human who reviews and can override every result.

Part 3: Bias against the communities we serve

AI repeats the inequities in its historical training data, and the people it hurts are the marginalized communities non-profits exist to serve. When training data carries the racism, language gaps, and class assumptions of the past, the model reproduces them. Vera Solutions' Fairness & Bias Mitigation principle says it plainly: assess AI systems for unintended bias "particularly regarding marginalized communities served by the organization" (Vera Solutions, 2024). Project Evident's Equitable AI Adoption framework (2026) makes "Safe and Fair Practice" one of its eight components and warns specifically against overreliance on automated systems.

Watch for these kinds of bias in program work:

Bias typeHow it shows upWho it harms
Racial / ethnicRisk scores, "needs" predictions, or matching that rate some groups worse on equal factsCommunities of color, over-surveilled groups
LanguageWorse output, mistranslation, or refusal for non-English or dialect speakersImmigrants, refugees, ESL beneficiaries
SocioeconomicModels trained mostly on better-resourced populations misread poverty as risk or fraudPeople in poverty, the under-documented
DisabilitySystems treat disability or mental-health topics as "dangerous," block or downgrade themPeople with disabilities, mental-health clients

The Allegheny family-screening case shows how bias hides in plain sight. The tool didn't predict child maltreatment directly. It predicted a proxy (home removal within two years), and critics argued the proxy itself encoded bias against poor and disabled parents (ACLU, 2024; Popular Science, 2024). The lesson: bias is rarely labeled "bias." It hides in proxies, in training data, and in who was missing from the data.

A scenario to work through. A housing non-profit pilots an AI "needs-assessment" assistant that ranks new clients by how urgently they need help. The model learned from five years of the organization's own intake-and-outcome records. It tests at 91 percent overall accuracy, and leadership is excited. But intake staff were mostly English-speaking when the historical data was collected, so Spanish-only clients have thinner, messier records. The model quietly scores them as lower urgency because their files look "incomplete." No one notices, because the 91 percent figure is an average that drowns the smaller group. A better average won't fix this; the bias check below will. Split the results by language, see the gap, and either remediate or do not deploy. This is the everyday shape of algorithmic harm in human services: a quiet, systematic under-serving of the people already hardest to reach, rather than a dramatic failure (Goldkind, Ming & Fink, 2024).

   BIAS CHECK — before deployment AND on a schedule
   1. Who is in the training data?  Who is MISSING?
   2. What is the model really predicting (the proxy)?  Is the proxy fair?
   3. Test on YOUR populations — split results by race / language / income / disability
   4. Is there a named human who reviews and can override every output?
   5. Who monitors this after launch, and how often?
        └─► fails any line → do not deploy / pause and remediate
Safeguard

Never deploy an AI tool that influences decisions about people until you have run the bias check (Exercise 2) and assigned someone to monitor it after launch. One test at the door is not enough: models drift, populations change, and harm accumulates quietly. Schedule the re-check.

Signal

Disaggregated results. A staffer who asks "does this work as well for our Spanish-speaking clients as for our English-speaking ones?" is practicing real Discernment, the 4D habit of evaluating output critically.

Noise

"We tested it and overall accuracy is 94 percent." Overall accuracy hides bias. A model can be 94 percent accurate overall and badly wrong for the one group you most need to protect.

4D tie-in—Discernment + Diligence: Discernment is spotting the bias; Diligence is documenting the check, owning the decision, and re-checking on a schedule.

Part 4: Trauma-informed consent and protecting sensitive data

A checkbox buried in a 12-page intake form is not consent for people who share information under stress, with limited choices, and sometimes after harm. That describes many people in your programs. The clinical-practice guidance is consistent: consent isn't a box checked at intake; it's a conversation you keep having. If a person learns later that an algorithm analyzed their trauma without their knowledge, that trust may never come back (Ensora Health, 2025; Blueprint, 2025; APA, 2025).

Before AI touches beneficiary data, trauma-informed and culturally appropriate consent means:

  • Plain language, the person's language. Explain what AI will do, what data it touches, why, and what they can refuse, in their language and at their literacy level.
  • Real choice, no penalty. A person can decline AI processing and still receive full service. If declining costs them service, it isn't consent.
  • Specific, not blanket. Existing intake consent almost never covers AI. Check whether you need new consent language (Assessment 7.4); you usually do.
  • Ongoing. Re-confirm when the use changes. Consent for translation is not consent for risk-scoring.
  • Culturally aware. Follow Vera's Ethics & Community Values principle: reflect the community's norms instead of a one-size template (Vera Solutions, 2024).

The cardinal rule from Module 2 applies with extra force here: never enter sensitive beneficiary data into public AI tools. Candid's wording makes a good staff test: don't enter "personally identifying or confidential information, legal documents, passwords, or anything you wouldn't paste into a public website" (Candid, 2025).

In plain terms…GDPR (if you serve people in the EU/UK)HIPAA (if you handle US health data)
What it protectsPersonal data; special category data (health, ethnicity, etc.) needs extra basisProtected health information (PHI)
For AI useLawful basis + purpose limitation + data minimization; people can objectA signed Business Associate Agreement with any vendor touching PHI; no PHI in non-compliant/public tools
Plain ruleCollect only what you need; tell people; let them say noIf a tool isn't covered by a BAA, PHI never goes in
Safeguard

Assume any free, consumer-grade tool (the public version of a popular chatbot) can retain and train on what you type. That makes it a do-not-enter zone for any identifiable beneficiary data. Real organizations have leaked confidential material exactly this way.

Practitioner note

Anonymizing is harder than it looks. A case note without a name can still hold enough to re-identify someone: a rare diagnosis, a specific town, an immigration date. When in doubt, keep it out of external tools and ask the data lead (Module 2).

Part 5: Human-in-the-loop, accessibility, and the concern escalation path

Human-in-the-loop for crisis and safety. Some decisions must always run through a person. Crisis-line workers studying AI in suicide prevention concluded that any AI involvement needs human supervision "particularly for high-risk cases". They were openly skeptical that an unsupervised system can weigh life-and-death ethical decisions (PMC, 2025). The danger is real: AI chatbots have impersonated 988, claimed to be the Lifeline, and handed out nonexistent crisis numbers (CrisisTalk, 2025; Futurism, 2025). Never route a person in crisis to an unsupervised AI. Keep the warm human handoff (a named person, a real number), and let AI assist staff only behind the scenes, e.g., by surfacing the correct, verified resource list for a human to use.

Accessibility—don't build a new barrier. AI can widen the digital divide. Tools learn from internet content that carries prejudice about disability, and they sometimes block disabled people's own conversations as "dangerous" (Rooted in Rights, 2024). Voice or chat tools can exclude people with limited literacy, no smartphone, low bandwidth, or a different home language. Assessment 7.6 asks you to review every beneficiary-facing tool for exactly these barriers.

   ACCESSIBILITY QUICK CHECK (beneficiary-facing AI)
   □ Works in the languages our people actually speak
   □ Readable at low literacy; offers a human alternative
   □ Usable without the newest phone / fast internet
   □ Doesn't penalize or block disability / mental-health topics
   □ A non-AI path exists for anyone who needs or wants it

The beneficiary-concern escalation path. People you serve must have a real way to ask "why is a computer involved in my case?" and to object (Assessment 7.5). This is an Organizational-dimension duty, and it works only if the path is named, posted, and staffed.

   BENEFICIARY RAISES A CONCERN
        │  (verbally, on a form, via an advocate — any channel)
        ▼
   FRONTLINE STAFF logs it, pauses any AI step on that case, reassures
        │
        ▼
   PROGRAM LEAD reviews within [X] business days; can override the AI
        │
        ▼
   AI/DATA OWNER (or ED) — pattern? fix the tool / consent / process
        │
        ▼
   RESPONSE to the person in plain language + log for the learning loop
Safeguard

Raising a concern must never reduce the service a person receives. If it does, people stop telling you when something is wrong, and you lose your single best early-warning system for harm.

Signal

Staff and beneficiaries helped write these rules. Vera's Ethics & Community Values principle and the TAG framework both insist that the community helps define acceptable use, because it surfaces cultural blind spots no internal team can see alone.

Noise

A concern mechanism that exists only on paper: a policy line no beneficiary has ever heard of and no staffer has ever used. An unused channel is the same as no channel.

The do-use lane (so the list isn't only "no"). AI is welcome on low-risk program admin that doesn't identify beneficiaries: drafting a public program FAQ, summarizing published research, formatting a public report, and translating general (non-personal) materials for human review. Frame AI as removing administrative burden, so staff spend more time with the people they serve.

Safeguard

Test the do-use lane with one question: if this output were wrong, who gets hurt? If the answer is "a staffer wastes ten minutes," it's likely a safe do-use. If the answer names a person you serve (their housing, safety, benefit, case, or dignity), it belongs on the do-not-use list, with a human firmly in charge.

Tie it back to the three dimensions. Everything in Part 5 maps cleanly. Human-in-the-loop is Technical (a control built into the workflow), accessibility is Technical and Ethical (fit and fairness), and the beneficiary-concern path is Organizational (accountability you can point to). Run every new AI idea through the same three questions. That habit, more than any single rule, keeps the people you serve safe over time.

Hands-on exercises

Exercise 1: Build (or adopt) your do-not-use list (25 min, small groups)

Start from the Part 2 table. In groups by program, add at least three program-specific rows, each a concrete task in your work where AI must not decide. For each row, write the task, the harm if it went wrong, and the do-use alternative AI could safely assist with.

Facilitator answer key—sample rows program staff commonly surface:

Program task (do NOT use AI to…)Harm if wrongSafe do-use alternative
Score which shelter applicants get a bed tonightA wrongly de-prioritized person sleeps outsideAI drafts the public intake-info sheet; human scores
Draft the message telling a family their case is closingRe-traumatizing tone; legal errorStaff writes it; AI checks public-info attachments only
Summarize a survivor's disclosure into a case noteLeak of identifying trauma dataStaff writes the note; no external tool touches it
Decide a client's immigration-relief eligibilityCatastrophic, irreversible harmAI summarizes published USCIS rules for the human

Exercise 2: Run a bias check before deployment (30 min, plenary then groups)

Take a real or proposed AI use that touches people (e.g., an intake-triage assistant) and walk through the Bias Check Worksheet (Templates section). The facilitator plays a vendor making confident claims, and groups must push past "94 percent accurate overall."

Facilitator answer key—what a passing check looks like:

  • Names who is in and missing from the training data.
  • States the real prediction/proxy and questions whether the proxy is fair (the Allegheny lesson).
  • Commits to disaggregated testing by race / language / income / disability on your population.
  • Names a human reviewer with override authority and a monitoring owner with a re-check date.
  • Red flag to fail on: "we can't see how it decides" + "no per-group results" → do not deploy.

Exercise 3: Write a trauma-informed consent script (20 min, pairs)

Use the Consent Guide (Templates) to draft a 4–6 sentence spoken script a caseworker could say before any AI touches a person's data. Then test it. Would someone in crisis, speaking their second language, understand it? Could they say no without losing service?

Facilitator sample solution:

Note

"Before we go further—to help me sort the paperwork faster, I'd like to use a computer tool to summarize only the non-private parts of your file. It will never see your [health/immigration/safety] details, and you can say no and still get every service, no questions asked. Do you have any questions? Is it okay with you if I use it—just for the paperwork?"

Key features: plain language, names what's excluded, explicit no-penalty refusal, invites questions, and specific to one use.

Knowledge check

  1. (MCQ) A funder deadline is tomorrow and a caseworker is behind. Which is acceptable?
    a) Paste the client's intake form into a free chatbot to summarize it
    b) Have AI decide which clients get the last three program slots
    c) Use AI to format a public program fact sheet, then review it
    d) Ask AI to write the message closing a family's case
  2. (Short answer) Name three categories on a non-profit do-not-use list and explain why each is high-stakes.
  3. (MCQ) A vendor says its triage tool is "94 percent accurate and bias-free." What is the best response?
    a) Approve it—that's a strong number
    b) Ask for results disaggregated by race, language, income, and disability, and who monitors it after launch
    c) Trust the "bias-free" claim
    d) Approve it if it's cheap
  4. (Short answer) Why is a checkbox in a 12-page intake form not trauma-informed consent for AI use?
  5. (MCQ) Which is required before AI processes EU beneficiaries' health data and US PHI, respectively?
    a) Nothing extra / nothing extra
    b) A lawful basis + minimization and the right to object / a Business Associate Agreement
    c) Board approval only / board approval only
    d) A vendor's "responsible AI" badge / the same
  6. (Short answer) A person in mental-health crisis contacts your program after hours. Why must they never be routed to an unsupervised AI? What should happen instead?
  7. (MCQ) A beneficiary asks why a computer is involved in their case. Under a good escalation path, what must NOT happen?
    a) Staff logs the concern
    b) Any AI step on the case is paused
    c) Their services are reduced because they complained
    d) A program lead reviews it
  8. (Short answer) Match each item to a dimension (Organizational / Ethical / Technical): (i) no consent was obtained; (ii) no human can override the tool; (iii) there's no way for a beneficiary to complain.
  9. (Short answer) Using the Allegheny example, explain the "proxy" problem.

Answer key

1 → c. 2 → e.g., eligibility decisions (denies help/compounds bias), crisis/trauma comms (re-traumatize/endanger), sensitive case notes (leak/legal duty), mental-health support (chatbots have given wrong crisis info), HR/monitoring (needs fair human judgment). 3 → b. 4 → consent must be plain-language, specific to the AI use, freely refusable without penalty, and an ongoing conversation; a buried checkbox meets none of these, and trust breaks if the person learns later. 5 → b. 6 → unsupervised chatbots have impersonated 988, given nonexistent crisis numbers, and can't weigh life-and-death ethics; instead, keep a warm human handoff (a named person and a verified crisis number), with AI only assisting staff behind the scenes. 7 → c. 8 → (i) Ethical, (ii) Technical, (iii) Organizational. 9 → the tool predicted a stand-in (home removal in two years) instead of harm itself; if the proxy encodes bias against poor/disabled parents, the "accurate" model still produces biased, harmful decisions.

Facilitator guide

Prep checklist

  • Pull your current intake and consent forms and any beneficiary-facing or decision-support tool in use, including informal or shadow tools. They make Exercises 1–3 real.
  • Print the four artifacts (do-not-use list, bias worksheet, consent guide, escalation one-pager) for each attendee.
  • Have the TAG + Project Evident framework and Vera's nine principles open as references.
  • Confirm that leadership or the board will approve the do-not-use list afterward (Module 1). This workshop drafts; governance ratifies.

Free and low-cost materials: TAG + Project Evident framework (tagtech.org/AI); Project Evident Equitable AI (projectevident.org); Vera Solutions nine principles; Candid responsible-AI-use guide (candid.org); NTEN. No paid tools required.

Timing: Protect the do-not-use build (Part 2/Exercise 1) and the bias check (Part 3/Exercise 2); they are the heart of the session. If you run late, trim Part 1, never these.

Common pitfalls

  • Turning it into a fear session. Pair every "no" with the do-use lane, and cite Candid's finding that ban-everything policies push AI underground.
  • Abstract debate. Keep pulling groups back to their own cases and forms.
  • Treating bias as a one-time door check. Press hard on the post-launch monitoring line.
  • Letting "the system said so" pass. Name it every time it appears in a scenario.

Discussion prompts

  • "Tell me about a decision in your work that a model should never make. What's the worst case if it did?"
  • "Where might our data be missing a group we serve?"
  • "How would one of our clients even know AI was involved—and how would they object?"

Tailoring by audience

  • A3 program/service staff (priority): full workshop; they own the do-not-use list and consent scripts.
  • A1/A2 leadership and board: attend Part 1 and the do-not-use readout; they ratify policy and own the Organizational dimension.
  • A6 IT/data lead: co-owns Part 3 (bias) and the Technical dimension, and runs post-launch monitoring.
  • All staff: a 45-min cut of Parts 2, 4, and 5 (the do-not-use list, the data rule, and the escalation path).

Addressing staff fear and resistance (a real non-profit barrier): Name the fear directly: "AI will hurt the people we serve" / "AI will replace me." Counter both honestly. The do-not-use list protects beneficiaries and staff judgment, and the do-use lane frees time for the people you serve. Co-create the rules so staff (and, where possible, beneficiaries) own them. Involvement surfaces cultural blind spots and makes the rules stick.
