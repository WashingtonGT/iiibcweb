Why this module exists

The headline number is brutal and clarifying: 95 percent of enterprise generative-AI pilots show zero measurable P&L return (MIT NANDA, 2025). The cause is weak integration and weaker measurement, not weak models. Most companies cannot tell you whether their AI spend is working because they're still measuring with instruments AI quietly broke. "Lines of code" rewards an AI that floods the repo. "Hours saved" is an estimate no CFO believes. Many organizations still rely on estimates rather than measured financial results, and some have no formal AI measurement framework at all.

Meanwhile, the cost side is quietly moving against you. Average monthly AI spend was projected to rise 36 percent in 2025, yet only 51 percent of organizations can confidently evaluate the ROI of that spend (CloudZero, 2025). Per-token prices fall while tokens-per-task rise, so total inference spend climbs. A prompt or model change can spike consumption 100× overnight. For an AI-embedded product, a handful of power users can compress a 75 percent gross margin toward 60 percent with no pricing change, and no one notices until the quarterly numbers land.

This module gives leadership the two instruments that make AI investment provable: a refreshed measurement stack that ties adoption to business outcomes, and AI unit economics that keep margin from leaking. Without these, as the parent plan states plainly, AI investment is unfalsifiable and unprovable.

Signal

The job of measurement is to answer three questions a board actually asks: Is it adopted? Is it making us better or faster? Is it making or losing us money net of its cost? Everything else is vanity.

Noise

"Our engineers say AI makes them 40 percent faster." Self-reported time savings, lines of AI-generated code, and pilot demos are activity metrics. They feel like progress (activity bloat: motion mistaken for progress) and prove nothing about the P&L.

Note

Research note (directional): Only ~39 percent of organizations report any measurable EBIT impact from AI and only ~5.5 percent attribute >5 percent of EBIT to it (McKinsey 2025). Workflow redesign—not model choice—is the strongest predictor of impact, yet ~75 percent haven't redesigned a workflow. Measurement is what reveals whether the redesign (Module G) actually paid.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

  1. Explain why old engineering/finance metrics fail under AI and name their replacements.
  2. Build the new measurement stack (AI code share, complexity-adjusted velocity, code turnover ratio, cost per ticket/feature, DX pulse).
  3. Apply the three-tier ROI model—adoption → depth/efficiency → financial impact—with token/usage cost always in the denominator.
  4. Track AI unit economics: cost per workflow, AI cost as % of COGS, contribution margin, and the power-user margin risk.
  5. Apply margin-protection levers (caching, batching, model routing, fine-tuning, usage-aligned pricing) and the L1–L5 cost-curve lens.
  6. Pair every velocity KPI with a quality/security guardrail so "more, faster" can't silently degrade reliability.
  7. Map each AI initiative to a board-reviewed business KPI and run an outcome-appropriate measurement cadence.
  8. Design a per-role AI dashboard and identify the red flag: an initiative that can't be tied to a real outcome.

Core content

Part 1: The new measurement stack

AI didn't just change productivity; it broke the metrics that used to measure it. Replace the left column with the right.

Old metricWhy AI broke itNew metric
Lines of codeAI inflates volume; more lines ≠ more valueAI code share (% AI-generated)
PR / raw velocityVolume up, complexity/value unclearComplexity-adjusted velocity
Defect countMisses code that's reworked or thrown awayCode turnover ratio (churn of recently shipped code)
Cost per engineerIgnores compute; headcount shifts to inferenceCost per ticket / cost per feature
Annual engagement surveyToo slow for AI-era changeQuarterly developer-experience (DX) pulse
Practitioner note

AI code share is a context metric, not a target. "40 percent of our lines are AI-generated" tells you adoption depth; it tells you nothing about whether that code is good. Always read it next to code turnover ratio and the quality guardrails (Part 4). A high AI code share with a high turnover ratio means the AI is producing code you keep rewriting: negative value dressed as productivity.

Part 2: The three-tier ROI model

Measure value in three ascending tiers. One discipline separates real ROI from fake: token/usage cost always goes in the denominator.

  TIER 3 — FINANCIAL IMPACT        ← revenue / margin, net of AI cost
        ▲   "Is it making or losing us money?"
        │   ROI with token+infra cost in the denominator
        │
  TIER 2 — DEPTH / EFFICIENCY      ← time saved, PRs/eng, cycle time, AI code share
        ▲   "Is it making us better or faster?"
        │
  TIER 1 — ADOPTION                ← active use/seat, depth-of-use, cert completions
            "Is it actually being used?"  (leading indicator only)
TierExample metricsBenchmark (directional)
1: AdoptionDaily/weekly active use, API calls, cert completions~51% of devs use AI daily, ~85% regularly
2: Depth / efficiencyTime saved, PRs/engineer, cycle-time delta, AI code share, % workflows in production vs pilotAI code share ~15–25% of lines (top quartile 40–60%); high adopters ~113% more PRs
3: Financial impactGross-margin impact, cost-to-serve, revenue/employee, ROI net of costHealthy AI-coding ROI ~2.5–3.5× (top quartile 4–6×) only when cost is in the denominator
Risk flag

Adoption is a leading indicator, not value. "90 percent of staff use AI weekly" with no Tier-2/3 movement is the BCG "adoption puzzle": usage up, impact flat. Never report Tier 1 alone to a board.

Part 3: AI unit economics and margin protection

For any AI in the product, treat it like a P&L line, not a feature.

Metrics to track (CFO-grade):

  • Cost per workflow / per ticket / per feature: not raw token count. Tokens are an input; cost-per-unit-of-value is the metric.
  • AI cost as % of COGS: the margin-erosion gauge.
  • Contribution margin by customer segment: surfaces the power-user problem.
  • Forecastability: can you predict next quarter's AI cost within a reasonable band? Most can't (only ~15 percent forecast within ±10 percent).

The power-user trap:

  Flat per-seat pricing + fat-tailed usage =
     light users subsidize heavy users until heavy users dominate.
  Illustrative: one account drifts from 75% → 60% gross margin (−15 pts)
     purely from heavier AI usage at unchanged price.

Margin-protection levers (teach these as first-class engineering and pricing decisions):

  1. Caching: don't pay twice for the same answer.
  2. Batching: amortize calls.
  3. Model routing: expensive reasoning models for planning/hard tasks only; cheap, fast models for bulk work (the Module D/E specialization lever).
  4. Fine-tuning / smaller models: for high-volume narrow tasks (50–70 percent cost reduction at scale).
  5. Usage-aligned pricing: meter or tier so heavy users pay for the cost they create.

The L1–L5 cost lens: engineering AI cost per head spans ~$20 (assisted) → ~$20,000 (full autonomy) per month, a ~1,000× range. Higher maturity trades headcount for inference. Workforce and margin planning must track this curve, not lag it.

Part 4: Velocity ↔ quality guardrails

Every velocity metric gets a paired quality metric. This is non-negotiable, because AI code shows materially more defects (~75 percent more logic errors, ~1.6× more security findings; CodeRabbit, December 2025). "More code, faster" with no guardrail is how you ship a security incident on schedule.

Velocity metricPaired guardrail
PRs / engineer ↑Defect rate / escaped bugs
AI code share ↑Code turnover ratio + escaped vulnerabilities
Cycle time ↓Review coverage on AI-generated code
Support deflection ↑ (CS)CSAT + escalation/false-resolution rate

Part 5: Map initiatives to KPIs and set a cadence

Two rules close the loop:

  1. Every material AI initiative maps to a board-reviewed business KPI. If it can't be mapped, that's the red flag: pause it or reframe it. This is the same discipline as the Module A portfolio.
  2. Cadence matches the outcome horizon. Review operational metrics (adoption, latency, cost, incidents) weekly or monthly, and value metrics (margin, revenue/employee, cycle time) quarterly. A monthly-only cadence misses outcomes that mature over quarters; a quarterly-only cadence misses a runaway cost spike for 90 days.
Signal

A balanced AI scorecard = 1–2 business-outcome metrics + 1–2 operational metrics + 1–2 quality/risk guardrails, each with a named owner and a cadence. That fits on one page and answers the board's three questions.

Knowledge check

  1. (MCQ) Why is "lines of code" a broken metric under AI? a) AI writes fewer lines · b) AI inflates volume, so more lines ≠ more value · c) It's illegal · d) It's too slow to collect
  2. (Short answer) Name the three tiers of the ROI model, in order.
  3. (MCQ) What must always go in the denominator of an AI ROI calculation? a) Headcount · b) Token/usage (inference) cost · c) Revenue · d) Lines of code
  4. (T/F) High weekly active usage of an AI tool proves it's delivering business value.
  5. (Short answer) Describe the power-user margin trap in one sentence.
  6. (Short answer) Name three margin-protection levers.
  7. (MCQ) A high AI code share combined with a high code turnover ratio most likely means: a) Great productivity · b) The AI is producing code you keep rewriting (negative value) · c) Low adoption · d) A security breach
  8. (Short answer) What's the "red flag" test for any AI initiative?

Answer key

Answer key: 1-b · 2: Adoption → Depth/efficiency → Financial impact · 3-b · 4-False (adoption is a leading indicator only; the BCG "adoption puzzle") · 5: flat per-seat pricing + fat-tailed usage means light users subsidize heavy users until heavy usage erodes gross margin · 6: any three of caching, batching, model routing, fine-tuning/small models, usage-aligned pricing · 7-b · 8: if it can't be mapped to a board-reviewed business KPI (you can't say what number moves if it fails), it's a story, not an investment. Instrument, reframe, or kill it.

Facilitator guide

Prep checklist:

  • Get the client's actual AI cost data (token/inference bills) and at least one product/feature P&L before the session. Abstract dashboards don't stick; their real numbers do.
  • Pull current adoption data (seat usage, API calls) and any existing engineering metrics so Lab M.1 uses live inputs.
  • Have Finance in the room: unit economics is a CFO conversation as much as an engineering one.

Timing: Half-day. Parts 1–3 are the core. If the client has no product-embedded AI, compress Part 3's product example and weight internal-productivity economics.

Common pitfalls:

  • Letting the room celebrate Tier-1 adoption as success → redirect to Tier 2/3 relentlessly.
  • Reporting ROI without cost in the denominator → the most common way AI ROI is overstated.
  • Treating measurement as an engineering-only or finance-only exercise → it's both; the dashboard needs joint owners.
  • Setting velocity targets with no paired quality guardrail → invites the "fast wrong code" failure.

Discussion prompts:

  • "Which of our AI initiatives could we kill tomorrow and not be able to prove we'd lost anything?"
  • "What's our AI cost as a % of COGS—and who owns that number?"
  • "Where is a power user quietly eating our margin?"

Tailoring by audience: Execs/Finance → Parts 2, 3, and 5 and the scorecard. Eng leadership → Parts 1 and 4 and the L1–L5 cost levers. PMs → Tier 2/3 for product features and the quality guardrails.
