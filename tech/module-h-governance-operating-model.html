Why This Module Exists

Every growth-stage technology company we meet has the same governance posture: nothing written down, and a quiet bet that nothing will go wrong before someone gets around to it. The bet is bad. Absence of governance is not "low overhead" — it is exactly the place where unmanaged risk concentrates. The fabricated statistic in a customer-facing report, the customer PII pasted into a public chatbot, the product feature that makes a consequential decision with no human able to override it — these are not edge cases. They are the default outcome of ungoverned adoption.

The cost of not doing this is asymmetric. A lightweight policy costs you an afternoon. A single AI-caused incident — a biased hiring screen, a hallucinated financial figure in a board deck, a data-residency breach in a regulated market — costs you a customer, an audit finding, or a headline. Worse, it costs you the right to keep using AI: the fastest way to get AI banned internally is to have one ungoverned failure that scares the board into a blanket freeze.

There is also an upside, and it is measurable. Hub-and-spoke ("hybrid") operating models deliver roughly 36% higher AI ROI than decentralized ones (IBM, directional). The reason is structural, not magical: a hybrid model gives you shared standards and tooling (the hub) plus domain ownership and speed (the spokes), so you avoid both the bottleneck of pure centralization and the chaos of pure federation. Governance done right is a return multiplier, not a tax.

The hard truth this module enforces: engineering cannot change the operating model alone. An AI Factory built without cost guardrails (finance), data-sovereignty rules (legal/security), and role realignment (HR) stalls — every time. Enterprise alignment is the gating factor. This session puts the right people in the room and produces the artifacts that keep them aligned.

Signal

Proportionate governance is a competitive asset — at assessment maturity 18–21 you are "ready for enterprise buyers and audits," which directly unblocks revenue.

Noise

A 40-page AI policy modeled on a Fortune-100 template. Nobody will read it, nobody will follow it, and it will be obsolete before legal finishes redlining it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, participants will be able to:

  1. Draft a proportionate one-page AI policy stating principles, roles, and approval gates — calibrated to the company's stage and data sensitivity, not copied from an enterprise template.
  2. Write an acceptable-use policy (AUP) that tells staff exactly what data may and may not enter third-party AI tools, and which tools/tiers are sanctioned.
  3. Build and seed a model inventory covering all AI systems — including third-party SaaS features and product-embedded models — each with purpose, risk tier, and a named human owner.
  4. Stand up a working AI risk register that scores likelihood × impact, names a mitigation and an owner, and is reviewed on a cadence.
  5. Define human-in-the-loop (HITL) rules for consequential decisions — specifying where a human must review, approve, or be able to override an AI-influenced outcome.
  6. Specify an incident detection and escalation path so that when an AI system produces a wrong or damaging output, people know exactly what to do.
  7. Choose an operating model (centralized vs. federated vs. hub-and-spoke) and charter a cross-functional AI council spanning engineering, security, legal, finance, and HR.
  8. Embed risk, safety, and bias as design inputs — wired into how new AI work starts, not bolted on as an end-of-line review.
  9. Locate accountability with a named human for every AI-influenced outcome, and articulate why accountability cannot be delegated to a model.

Core Content

Part 1 — Proportionate Governance: The Seven Artifacts

Governance for a 50–500 person technology company is not enterprise compliance theater. It is a small set of artifacts that are lightweight but real — short enough that people actually use them, real enough that they change behavior. Section 5 of the assessment defines exactly seven things, and this module produces or drafts each:

THE SEVEN GOVERNANCE ARTIFACTS (Assessment Section 5)
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ 1. AI Policy            principles, roles, approval gates     │
│ 2. Acceptable-Use Policy what data goes in which tools        │
│ 3. Model Inventory       every AI system: purpose/tier/owner  │
│ 4. AI Risk Register      likelihood × impact, mitigation, owner│
│ 5. Human-in-the-Loop     where humans must review/override    │
│ 6. Incident & Escalation what to do when AI fails             │
│ 7. Operating Model       centralized/federated/hub-and-spoke  │
│    + Risk/safety/bias as DESIGN INPUTS (cross-cutting)        │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Proportionality is the governing principle. The right amount of governance is a function of two variables: your stage (how many people, how fast you ship) and your data sensitivity (PII, PHI, financials, source code, regulated data). A 60-person SaaS with no regulated data needs a one-page policy and a shared inventory spreadsheet. A 300-person healthtech touching PHI needs the same artifacts plus stricter HITL rules and a mapped compliance overlay. The artifacts are the same; the stringency dial moves.

Practitioner note

I have watched two failure modes kill governance. The first is overkill — a borrowed enterprise policy so heavy that engineering routes around it and the document becomes fiction. The second is vapor — "we have principles" with nothing written, no owner, no inventory. Both score zero in practice. The win condition is a living one-pager plus a maintained spreadsheet that a new hire can read in ten minutes.

Do / Don't for the AI Policy:

DoDon't
Keep it to one page; principles + roles + gatesWrite a 40-page document nobody reads
Name a single accountable owner (CAIO/CTO)Leave accountability diffuse ("the team")
Define which use cases need approval, by riskRequire approval for everything (creates shadow AI)
Version it and set a quarterly review dateTreat it as write-once and forget
Tie gates to data sensitivity and consequenceGate by tool brand name (tools change weekly)

Part 2 — Model Inventory & AI Risk Register

You cannot govern what you cannot see. The single highest-leverage artifact for a growth-stage company is the model inventory — because most teams genuinely do not know how many AI systems they operate.

What counts as an "AI system" in the inventory. This is where teams under-count. The inventory must include:

  • Systems you build — your own RAG app, fine-tuned model, internal agent.
  • Third-party / SaaS-embedded AI — the AI features inside tools you already pay for (the summarizer in your support desk, the AI in your CRM, the assistant in your code editor). These are AI systems you operate, even though you didn't train them.
  • Product-embedded models — any AI feature you ship to your customers. These carry the highest external risk.
  • Shadow AI you discover — the unsanctioned ChatGPT tab. Once found, it goes in the inventory and gets a decision: sanction, replace, or retire.

For each system, capture purpose, risk tier, and a named human owner at minimum. Risk-tier with a simple, defensible scale you can later map to the EU AI Act in Module L:

RISK TIER (simple internal scale — maps forward to EU AI Act in Module L)
  CRITICAL  influences consequential decisions about people, money, safety,
            or legal rights (hiring, credit, clinical, eligibility)
  HIGH      customer-facing output, or touches sensitive data (PII/PHI/financials)
  MEDIUM    internal productivity with material business impact
  LOW       internal convenience, low-consequence, no sensitive data

The AI risk register is the companion. Where the inventory lists what you run, the register lists what could go wrong and who owns the fix. Score each risk on likelihood × impact, name a concrete mitigation, assign an owner, and set a review date. Keep it to the risks that are real for your systems — bias in a screening model, hallucination in a customer-facing summary, data leakage to a third-party tool, prompt injection in an agent, cost runaway in an unmetered workflow.

Risk flag

The most common gap in growth-stage inventories is product-embedded AI shipped by a fast-moving product team with no risk tier and no owner. That is the system most likely to harm a customer and the one regulators and enterprise buyers will ask about first. Inventory it before you ship the next feature.

Do / Don't for inventory & register:

DoDon't
Include third-party and product-embedded AIOnly list models you trained yourself
Assign one named human owner per rowUse a team name as the owner
Score risk likelihood × impact, simplyBuild a 25-field NIST-grade taxonomy on day one
Review on a cadence (monthly/quarterly)Fill it once and let it rot

Part 3 — Human-in-the-Loop Rules & Incident Escalation

Human-in-the-loop (HITL) rules answer one question for each consequential decision: where must a human review, approve, or be able to override an AI-influenced outcome? Consequential means it affects a person's money, rights, safety, employment, health, or a material business commitment.

There are three levels of human involvement — name which applies per decision type:

HITL LEVELS
  HUMAN-IN-THE-LOOP    AI proposes, a human must approve before action
                       (e.g., AI drafts a customer refund > $X; human approves)
  HUMAN-ON-THE-LOOP    AI acts; a human monitors and can intervene/override
                       (e.g., AI auto-triages tickets; human can reclassify)
  HUMAN-OUT-OF-LOOP    AI acts autonomously; allowed ONLY for low-consequence,
                       reversible, low-sensitivity actions

The rule of thumb: the higher the consequence and the harder to reverse, the more the dial moves toward in-the-loop. A hiring screen, a credit decision, a clinical suggestion, anything touching legal rights — human-in-the-loop, no exceptions, with the override path documented.

Incident detection and escalation closes the loop. When an AI system produces a wrong or damaging output, people must know what to do before it happens, not improvise during it. This was Diagnostic Question 2 for a reason — most companies have no answer. A minimum viable escalation path:

AI INCIDENT ESCALATION (minimum viable)
  DETECT    who/what notices? (user report, monitor, eval failure, drift alert)
     │
  TRIAGE    on-call owner assesses severity (cosmetic → harmful → systemic)
     │
  CONTAIN   disable feature / fall back to human / revoke tool access
     │
  ESCALATE  Sev-1 (customer harm, data leak, legal exposure) → AI council + exec
     │
  LEARN     post-incident note → new risk-register entry → policy/HITL update
Signal

The escalation path is the artifact that turns a near-miss into a one-line risk-register update instead of a crisis. It is also the single best answer to give an enterprise buyer's security questionnaire.

Part 4 — Choosing the Operating Model & the AI Council

Three operating models exist for organizing AI work. The choice determines your speed, your consistency, and your ROI.

                CENTRALIZED        FEDERATED          HUB-AND-SPOKE
                (one AI team)      (each BU on its    (shared hub +
                                    own)               domain spokes)
 Speed          slow (bottleneck)  fast (no friction) fast where it matters
 Consistency    high               low (sprawl)       high (shared standards)
 Domain fit     weak               strong             strong
 Duplication    none               high               low
 Best for       very early /       large, autonomous  GROWTH-STAGE TECH
                high-risk only     divisions          (most clients)
  • Centralized — one AI team owns everything. Great control, terrible throughput; the central team becomes the bottleneck the moment demand grows. Fine for the first 90 days or for a single high-risk regulated workload.
  • Federated — every business unit does its own thing. Fast and domain-aware, but you get tool sprawl, duplicated spend, inconsistent risk posture, and no shared learning. This is what "ungoverned bottom-up adoption" decays into.
  • Hub-and-spoke (hybrid) — a small central hub owns shared standards, tooling, the model inventory, the risk register, security review, and the governance artifacts; spokes (embedded practitioners in each domain) own delivery and move fast within the guardrails. This combination is why hybrid delivers ~36% higher ROI (IBM, directional): you get federation's speed without its chaos and centralization's standards without its bottleneck.

For a 50–500 person technology company, hub-and-spoke is the default recommendation. The hub may be one person (often the CAIO or a CTO-deputy) plus a part-time council; the spokes are the engineers and PMs already building.

The cross-functional AI council is the connective tissue of the hub. It must span five functions — and the absence of any one is a predictable failure mode:

THE AI COUNCIL — five seats, five reasons
  ENGINEERING   builds it; owns technical feasibility and the platform
  SECURITY      owns threat model, data flows, OWASP-LLM controls
  LEGAL         owns regulatory exposure, contracts, IP, data residency
  FINANCE       owns cost guardrails and unit economics (cost per workflow)
  HR / PEOPLE   owns role realignment, training, and people-impact of AI
  (+ chaired by the accountable AI owner — CAIO/CTO)
Practitioner note

The seat companies forget is finance, and it is the one that kills AI Factories. An engineering team can build a brilliant agent that costs $4 per invocation in tokens and quietly destroys the unit economics of the workflow it was meant to improve. No finance seat, no cost guardrail, no sustainable program. (Module M arms this seat with the metric stack.)

Enterprise alignment is the gating factor. Engineering moving alone is the failure pattern this module exists to prevent. The council exists so procurement, security, legal, finance, and HR move along the same maturity curve as engineering — not three quarters behind it. A spoke can ship fast precisely because the hub has already aligned the enterprise on the guardrails.

Part 5 — Risk as Design Input & Accountability Stays Human

Two principles cut across every artifact above.

Risk, safety, and bias are design inputs, not bolt-on reviews. The bolt-on pattern — build the thing, then ask security/legal to bless it at the end — guarantees friction, rework, and resentment, and it catches problems when they are most expensive to fix. Instead, wire risk into how AI work starts: a new AI initiative opens with three questions answered up front — What is the risk tier? What sensitive data does it touch? Where does a human stay in the loop? This is a five-minute intake at kickoff, not a gate at the finish line. It is also IIIBC's stated stance: risk-first, not risk-after.

BOLT-ON (don't)              DESIGN INPUT (do)
  build ──► ship ──► review     intake (tier/data/HITL)
                       │           │
                       ▼           ▼
                    rework      build within guardrails
                                    │
                                    ▼
                                 ship + register entry

Accountability for outcomes stays human. A model is not accountable for anything — it cannot be fired, sued, or held responsible. For every AI-influenced outcome there must be a named human who owns the result. "The AI decided" is never an acceptable answer to a customer, a regulator, or a board. This is why every inventory row, every register entry, and every HITL rule names a person, not a system. The model is a tool; the accountability is human, always.

Signal

When a board member asks "who is accountable if the AI gets it wrong?", the right answer is a name. If your answer is a job title with no person in it, or "the vendor," you have a governance gap, not a governance posture.

Hands-On Lab / Exercises

The lab is the point of the session. By the end, the team has real artifacts for real systems — not exercises with toy data.

Lab Setup (5 min)

Before the lab, the facilitator asks the group to name three real AI systems the company actually runs. Pick a spread: ideally one product-embedded, one third-party SaaS feature, and one internal tool. (If the team is stuck, the answer key below uses three realistic growth-stage examples.)

Exercise 1 — Draft the One-Page AI Policy (20 min)

Using the template in the next section, fill the one-page AI policy as a group:

  1. Write 4–6 principles (in IIIBC voice: signal over noise, risk-first, accountability human).
  2. Name the accountable owner and the AI council seats.
  3. Define the approval gates: which risk tiers require council review before launch.
  4. Write the AUP core rule: what data may and may not enter third-party tools, and which tools are sanctioned.
  5. Set a review cadence and version it.

Exercise 2 — Seed the Model Inventory (15 min)

For each of the three named systems, add a row to the model inventory template: name, type (built / third-party / product-embedded), purpose, risk tier, sensitive data touched, named owner, HITL level.

Exercise 3 — Seed the Risk Register (15 min)

For each system, identify the single most important risk, add it to the register: description, likelihood (L/M/H), impact (L/M/H), resulting priority, mitigation, owner, review date. Then assign the operating-model decision (likely hub-and-spoke) and confirm every artifact has a named human owner.

Knowledge Check

  1. (MCQ) Which operating model delivers roughly 36% higher AI ROI for a growth-stage company, and why?
    • a) Centralized — total control
    • b) Federated — maximum speed
    • c) Hub-and-spoke — shared standards (hub) plus domain speed (spokes)
    • d) No model — governance reduces ROI
  2. (Short answer) Name the five functional seats on the cross-functional AI council, and state which one is most often forgotten and what it prevents.
  3. (MCQ) A third-party support tool's AI summarizer touches customer PII. Does it belong in your model inventory?
    • a) No — you didn't build it
    • b) No — it's just a feature
    • c) Yes — it's an AI system you operate; inventory it with a tier and owner
    • d) Only if it's customer-facing
  4. (Short answer) Define the difference between human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, and human-out-of-the-loop, and give one decision type that must be in-the-loop.
  5. (MCQ) "Risk as a design input" means:
    • a) A security review at the end of the build
    • b) Answering risk-tier / sensitive-data / HITL questions at kickoff
    • c) Buying a risk-scanning tool
    • d) Adding a disclaimer to AI outputs
  6. (Short answer) A board member asks, "Who is accountable if our AI gets it wrong?" What kind of answer signals a healthy governance posture, and what answer signals a gap?
  7. (MCQ) Which is the correct order of the incident escalation path?
    • a) Escalate → Detect → Contain → Learn → Triage
    • b) Detect → Triage → Contain → Escalate → Learn
    • c) Contain → Detect → Learn → Escalate → Triage
    • d) Learn → Detect → Triage → Escalate → Contain
  8. (Short answer) Why is "engineering cannot change the operating model alone" the gating idea of this module? Name two functions whose absence stalls an AI program.
  9. (MCQ) The right amount of governance for a company is primarily a function of:
    • a) Headcount alone
    • b) Stage and data sensitivity
    • c) The longest competitor's policy
    • d) How many AI tools are purchased

Answer key

  1. c. Hub-and-spoke combines a standards-owning hub with fast domain spokes — speed without chaos, standards without bottleneck (IBM, directional ~36%).
  2. Engineering, Security, Legal, Finance, HR/People. Finance is most forgotten; its absence means no cost guardrails — agents that wreck unit economics and stall the program.
  3. c. It is an AI system you operate. Third-party and product-embedded AI both go in the inventory with a risk tier and a named owner.
  4. In-the-loop: AI proposes, human approves before action. On-the-loop: AI acts, human monitors and can override. Out-of-loop: AI acts autonomously (only low-consequence, reversible, low-sensitivity). Must-be-in-the-loop: hiring screen / credit decision / clinical suggestion / any consequential decision about a person.
  5. b. Risk/safety/bias are wired into how AI work starts (a kickoff intake), not bolted on at the end.
  6. Healthy: a named human owns the outcome. Gap: "the AI decided," "the vendor," or a job title with no person in it. Accountability stays human.
  7. b. Detect → Triage → Contain → Escalate → Learn.
  8. Because an AI Factory built without finance (cost guardrails), legal/security (data sovereignty), or HR (role realignment) stalls — enterprise alignment is the gating factor. Two of: Finance, Legal, Security, HR.
  9. b. Stage and data sensitivity. The artifacts are constant; the stringency dial moves.

Facilitator Guide

Prep checklist:

  • Confirm the right people are in the room: an executive with authority, plus security, legal, finance, HR, and an eng/data lead. If finance or HR is missing, the operating-model and accountability sections will produce hollow artifacts — push to get them there.
  • Have the participants' Section 5 score ready; open by reading it back to them.
  • Ask each attendee in advance to bring one real AI system they know the company runs (primes Exercise 1).
  • Print or share Templates 1–4 as editable docs (shared spreadsheet for inventory + register works best).

Materials needed: the four templates (editable), the Section 5 results, a shared screen for live editing, the council charter starter.

Timing guidance: Protect the lab — it is where the value is. If you run long in teaching, compress Part 4's operating-model discussion (most growth-stage clients land on hub-and-spoke) rather than the lab. Aim to leave the room with at least the one-page policy and three inventory + register rows committed with named owners and dates.

Common pitfalls:

  • Enterprise-template gravity — someone wants to import a 40-page policy. Redirect to proportionality; the one-pager is the deliverable.
  • Owner = team — push for a named person on every artifact, every time.
  • Under-counting AI — teams forget third-party and product-embedded AI. Probe: "What AI is in the tools you already pay for? What AI do we ship to customers?"
  • Risk-tier by vibe — tier by consequence and data, not by how casual the tool feels (see answer-key reframing).
  • No review date — an artifact with no cadence is already dead. Assign the next review before close.

Discussion prompts:

  • "Walk me through what happens today when an AI output is wrong. Who finds out? Who fixes it?" (Surfaces the missing incident path.)
  • "Which of our AI systems makes a decision a customer would be upset to learn was automated?" (Surfaces CRITICAL tier + HITL gaps.)
  • "If we froze all AI use tomorrow because of one incident, what would that cost us?" (Reframes governance as protecting the right to keep using AI.)

Tailoring by audience:

  • Heavily regulated (fintech/healthtech/legaltech): tighten HITL defaults, add the Module L compliance overlay, and expect a fuller risk register.
  • Low-sensitivity SaaS: keep it genuinely light — one page, one shared sheet, monthly council.
  • Board-heavy room: lead harder on the accountability-stays-human and 36% ROI framing; lighter on artifact mechanics (delegate those to the owners).
