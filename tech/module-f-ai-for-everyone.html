Why This Module Exists

A productivity gap is opening inside companies, not just between them. Only about 6% of employees describe themselves as "very comfortable" using AI, and roughly one in three are actively uncomfortable. Meanwhile a small set of self-taught power users compound their advantage every week. Left alone, this hardens into a two-track workforce: AI-haves who move faster and AI-have-nots who fall behind — inside the same team, on the same comp band.

The cause is not talent. It is training. Only about 12% of employees received any AI training in 2024, yet 48% say they would use AI more if they got formal training. The demand is already there; the floor is just too low. When you skip deliberate enablement, people either avoid the tools (and lose the productivity you paid for) or use them recklessly (and put customer-facing accuracy and confidential data at risk).

For a growth-stage operator, GTM is where this bites first. Sales reps draft outreach, CS agents answer customers, marketers ship content — all day, at volume, often touching customer data. A confident-but-wrong AI answer here is not an internal mistake; it reaches a prospect, a customer, or the public.

Signal

Talent density is built deliberately. Raising the floor — getting the uncomfortable third to genuine Practitioner fluency — usually returns more than coaching the power users to be slightly faster.

Noise

"Just give everyone a ChatGPT login and they'll figure it out." Access is not adoption. The 2024 data is unambiguous: tool access without training produces a divide, not fluency.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, participants will be able to:

  1. Write a structured prompt using a repeatable pattern (Role · Task · Context · Format · Constraints) for everyday work — drafting, summarizing, research, analysis, and meeting notes.
  2. Apply role-specific prompt patterns for their function (sales, marketing, or customer success) to a real work task.
  3. Spot a likely hallucination or bias in an AI output and name the verification step they will take before it reaches a customer.
  4. State the acceptable-use rules — specifically what data must never go into an AI tool — without looking them up.
  5. Run a human-review pass on any customer-facing AI draft, using a checklist, before it leaves the building.
  6. Build and maintain a personal prompt library of their five most-used, proven prompts.
  7. Turn a weak prompt into a strong one by diagnosing what was missing (context, role, format, or constraint).
  8. Describe how their manager's weekly nudge works and what reps are expected to contribute back to the team library.

Core Content

Part 1 — How to Talk to AI (the R-T-C-F-C Pattern)

Most weak AI results come from weak instructions, not a weak model. The fix is a pattern you can remember on a busy day. We call it R-T-C-F-C:

ROLE       — who you want the AI to act as       ("You are a B2B SaaS sales researcher.")
TASK        — the one thing you want done          ("Summarize this prospect's recent news.")
CONTEXT     — what it needs to know to do it well  ("We sell fraud-detection to fintechs.")
FORMAT      — the shape of the answer you want      ("5 bullets, each under 15 words.")
CONSTRAINTS — the rules and limits                  ("Only use the text I paste. No guessing.")

You do not need all five every time. But the more a task matters, the more of them you should include. The single biggest upgrade for non-technical users is adding Context and Format — that is where vague, generic output turns into something usable.

Do / Don't

DoDon't
Give the AI a role and an audienceAssume it knows your company or product
Paste the source text you want it to useAsk it to "look up" recent facts it can't verify
Ask for a specific format (bullets, table, 100 words)Accept a wall of text and edit it for 20 minutes
Tell it what NOT to do ("don't invent stats")Trust the first draft for anything customer-facing
Iterate: "make it shorter / warmer / more specific"Re-type the whole prompt to make one change
Practitioner note

Treat the AI like a sharp new hire on day one — capable, fast, eager, and completely ignorant of your business. You would never let that person email a customer unsupervised. Same rule here.

Part 2 — Everyday Wins (Drafting, Summarizing, Research, Analysis, Notes)

These five tasks cover most of a non-technical person's AI use. Each has a reliable pattern.

Drafting. Give the AI a role, the audience, the key points, and the tone. Always edit the result — the draft is a starting line, not a finish line.

Note

"You are a CS specialist. Draft a reply to a customer frustrated about a billing error. Apologize, explain we've issued a refund, offer a 15-min call. Warm, plain English, under 120 words. Don't promise anything beyond the refund and the call."

Summarizing. Paste the text and ask for a specific shape. This is the safest, highest-value everyday use because the AI works only from text you provide.

Note

"Summarize the pasted call transcript into: (1) what the customer wants, (2) objections raised, (3) agreed next steps. Bullets. Quote them directly where it matters."

Research (with a hard caveat). AI is good at organizing what you give it and generating angles to investigate. It is not a reliable source of current facts, names, numbers, or citations. Use it to structure thinking, then verify every fact yourself.

Note

"I'm preparing for a call with a healthtech CFO. List 8 questions a fraud-detection vendor should ask to understand their priorities. Don't state facts about any specific company."

Analysis. Paste structured data (a list, a table, exported numbers) and ask for patterns, not predictions. Spot-check the math.

Note

"Here are 20 closed-lost reasons from last quarter. Group them into themes, count each, and name the top 3. Use only the rows I pasted."

Meeting notes. Turn a messy transcript or your rough notes into structured output: decisions, owners, deadlines, open questions. Then confirm owners and dates against your own memory before sharing.

RAW INPUT                    AI TRANSFORM                 HUMAN GATE
messy transcript    ──►   decisions / owners / dates   ──►  confirm + send
exported numbers    ──►   themes + counts              ──►  spot-check math
long thread         ──►   3-bullet summary             ──►  verify facts
Signal

The tasks where AI is safest are the ones where it works only from text you give it (summarize, reformat, restructure). The tasks where it's riskiest are the ones where it generates facts (research, stats, names). Bias your everyday use toward the first column.

Part 3 — Your Role's Playbook

AI helps most when it's pointed at the specific, repeated work of your role. The full pattern library is in the Takeaway Artifacts; here is the principle for each GTM function. Every customer-facing output gets a human review pass — no exceptions.

Sales. Use AI for preparation and drafting, never for facts about the customer.

  • Account research: structure your prep, generate smart discovery questions, organize notes you already have. Verify any company fact independently.
  • Outreach drafting: turn your bullet points and a value prop into a first-draft email or LinkedIn message. You supply the specifics; AI supplies the structure and tone. You edit for truth and voice.
  • Call prep: summarize prior call notes into talking points, likely objections, and next-step options.

Marketing. Use AI for volume and variation, with a human owning the claim and the brand voice.

  • Content drafts: outlines, first drafts, headline variations, subject lines. Treat as raw material.
  • Repurposing: turn one approved asset (a blog, a webinar transcript) into many formats (social posts, email, FAQ). This is one of the highest-ROI uses because the source is already vetted.
  • Hard rule: any product claim, stat, or customer reference is verified against an approved source before publishing.

Customer Success. Use AI for response drafting and knowledge retrieval, with accuracy owned by the human.

  • Response drafting: turn a resolution into a clear, on-tone reply. Faster, more consistent, still reviewed.
  • Knowledge retrieval: summarize and explain from your own docs you paste in (or a sanctioned, grounded internal tool). Never let it invent a policy, price, or SLA.
  • Hard rule: if the AI states a specific number (refund amount, SLA, price, entitlement), confirm it against the system of record before sending.
Risk flag

Customer-facing accuracy is the whole game in GTM. A hallucinated stat in a marketing post, a wrong SLA in a CS reply, or an invented "fact" about a prospect in a sales email all cost trust that took months to build. The human-review gate is not bureaucracy — it's the difference between a tool and a liability.

Part 4 — When AI Lies (Hallucination, Bias, and the Review Gate)

AI models are built to produce plausible text, not true text. Most of the time plausible and true overlap. The danger is the gap — and it's stated with total confidence.

Hallucination = the AI states something false as if it were fact. Common forms: invented statistics, fake citations or URLs, plausible-but-wrong names/dates, made-up product features or policies.

Bias = the AI reflects skews in its training data: stereotyped assumptions, one-sided framing, defaulting to a dominant perspective. In GTM this shows up as tone-deaf copy, assumptions about who the buyer is, or unbalanced messaging.

How to catch it — the smell test:

Warning signWhat to do
A specific number, date, or stat you can't traceDon't use it until you verify the source
A citation, link, or "study"Open it. Confirm it exists and says that.
A confident claim about your product/policy/priceCheck the system of record
A fact about a specific company or personVerify independently — assume it's a guess
Output that feels one-sided or stereotypedRe-prompt for balance; have a colleague glance

The human-review gate — run this before anything reaches a customer:

   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  BEFORE IT LEAVES THE BUILDING                │
   │  □ Every fact/number/claim verified            │
   │  □ Sounds like us (brand/voice)                │
   │  □ No confidential data was used in the prompt │
   │  □ A human owns this — that human is me        │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
Practitioner note

You don't have to verify everything you ever generate — a brainstorm for your eyes only is low-stakes. You do have to verify everything that (a) reaches a customer or the public, or (b) feeds a decision. The stakes set the scrutiny.

Part 5 — The Rules and Your Prompt Library

Acceptable use — what must never go into a public AI tool. This is the short version of the company AUP (full detail in Module K). Commit it to memory:

Never paste inWhy
Customer records / personal data (PII, PHI)Privacy law, contracts, trust
Confidential financials, deal terms, pricing strategyCompetitive and contractual harm
Source code or proprietary IPLeaks the moat
Anything under NDA or marked confidentialLegal exposure
Credentials, API keys, secretsSecurity breach

The rule of thumb: if you'd be uncomfortable seeing it on a competitor's screen, don't paste it into a tool you don't control. Use only sanctioned tools with verified training-opt-out (your manager or Module K tells you which). When in doubt, ask before you paste.

Build a personal prompt library. The fastest path from Awareness to Practitioner is stopping the re-invention. Every time a prompt works well, save it.

  • Keep it simple: a doc, a notes app, or a shared team sheet.
  • Capture the prompt, what it's for, and one note on what made it work.
  • Aim for your top 5 first. Quality over quantity.
  • Contribute your best one to the team library each week (this is what the manager nudge collects).
Signal

A shared team prompt library is the single most effective floor-raiser. It turns one person's discovery into everyone's default, and it's how the uncomfortable third gets a running start instead of a blank page.

Part 6 — Manager-Led Adoption Nudges

Training events fade. Weekly reps stick. Formal, manager-led enablement is the single biggest driver of sustained AI adoption — far more than any one workshop. Managers run a light, repeating ritual for four weeks after the live session:

WEEKLY NUDGE (15 min, in an existing team meeting)
  1. SHARE  — one person demos a prompt that saved them time
  2. STEAL  — everyone saves it to their library
  3. REP    — each person commits to one applied use this week
  4. ASK    — surface one place AI didn't work; troubleshoot together

This is covered in depth for managers in Module G. For participants, the expectation is simple: show up with one win and one question each week.

Noise

"We did the training, so adoption is handled." A single session moves Awareness. Only repeated, manager-modeled practice moves Practitioner. Budget the four weeks of nudges as part of the module — not as optional follow-up.

Hands-On Lab / Exercises

Exercise 1 — Fix the Weak Prompt (15 min)

Below is a weak prompt. Rewrite it using R-T-C-F-C. Then run both and compare.

Weak: Write a sales email.

Your task: add a Role, a Task, real Context (use your actual product), a Format, and at least one Constraint.

Sample strong rewrite (facilitator key):

Note

"You are an SDR for a fraud-detection SaaS that sells to fintechs. Draft a cold email to a VP of Risk at a Series-C lending startup. Hook on rising chargeback fraud in lending. Format: under 90 words, one clear CTA (15-min call), no jargon. Don't invent stats about their company or ours; leave a [bracket] where I should add a real number."

Why it's better: role + audience set the tone; context makes it relevant; format keeps it short and scannable; the constraint prevents hallucinated stats and flags where the human must supply a real number.

Exercise 2 — Run the Review Gate on a Real Draft (20 min)

  1. Pick a real, low-risk task from your role (a follow-up email, a summary, a content outline).
  2. Generate a draft with a strong prompt.
  3. Run the human-review gate checklist line by line.
  4. Find at least one thing to fix or verify. Write down what it was.

Facilitator key — what people usually find: an unverifiable claim or number; a tone that's off-brand; a detail the AI invented; or a near-miss where confidential data almost went into the prompt. The goal is to make the gate a reflex, not to produce a perfect draft.

Exercise 3 — Start Your Prompt Library (10 min)

  1. Take the best prompt you wrote today.
  2. Save it in your library with: the prompt, what it's for, one note on why it worked.
  3. Post your single best prompt to the team library.

Facilitator key: success = every participant leaves with at least one saved prompt and one contribution to the shared sheet. This seeds the Week-1 nudge.

Artifact E — The "Can I Paste This?" Quick Check

Before pasting into any AI tool, ask:
  □ Is this a SANCTIONED tool (training opt-out verified)?
  □ Customer/personal data?  → NO. Stop.
  □ Confidential financials / deal terms / pricing?  → NO. Stop.
  □ Source code / proprietary IP?  → NO. Stop.
  □ Under NDA or marked confidential?  → NO. Stop.
  □ Would I mind a competitor seeing it?  → If yes, don't paste.
When in doubt → ask before you paste.

Knowledge Check

  1. (MCQ) What do the letters R-T-C-F-C stand for in the prompt pattern?
    • a) Read, Type, Check, Format, Confirm
    • b) Role, Task, Context, Format, Constraints
    • c) Request, Tone, Content, Feedback, Close
  2. (MCQ) Which everyday task is safest for AI because it works only from text you provide?
    • a) Researching a prospect's latest funding round
    • b) Summarizing a transcript you paste in
    • c) Stating your product's current SLA from memory
  3. (Short answer) A draft marketing post cites "a 2024 study showing 73% of buyers prefer X." What two things must you do before publishing?
  4. (MCQ) A teammate wants to paste a customer's account record into ChatGPT to draft a reply. What's the correct response?
    • a) Fine, it's just one record
    • b) Stop — customer/personal data must never go into a public AI tool
    • c) Only if they delete it afterward
  5. (Short answer) Why is "repurposing" one of the highest-ROI AI uses for marketing — and what's the one rule that keeps it safe?
  6. (MCQ) What is a "hallucination" in AI output?
    • a) A creative idea the AI generates
    • b) A false statement presented confidently as fact
    • c) A formatting error
  7. (Short answer) Rewrite this weak prompt into a strong one for your role: "Write something for a customer."
  8. (MCQ) What is the single biggest driver of sustained AI adoption on a team?
    • a) A one-time training session
    • b) Giving everyone a tool license
    • c) Repeated, manager-led weekly nudges and modeling
  9. (Short answer) Name the four checks on the human-review gate before a draft reaches a customer.
  10. (Short answer) What goes into your personal prompt library, and how often should you contribute to the team library?

Answer key

  1. b — Role, Task, Context, Format, Constraints.
  2. b — Summarizing pasted text; the AI isn't generating new facts.
  3. Verify the study exists and actually says that (open the source), and confirm the statistic is current/accurate — or remove it. Don't publish an untraceable stat.
  4. b — Customer/personal data must never go into a public AI tool.
  5. The source asset is already approved/vetted, so the facts are trustworthy; AI just reshapes it into many formats. Rule: keep all claims exactly as written in the approved source — don't let the AI add or alter claims.
  6. b — A false statement presented confidently as fact.
  7. Any answer with a clear role, task, real context, format, and at least one constraint (e.g., "You are a CS specialist; turn this resolution [paste] into a warm reply under 120 words; state only facts in the resolution").
  8. c — Repeated, manager-led weekly nudges and modeling.
  9. Every fact/number/claim verified; sounds like us (brand/voice); no confidential data used in the prompt; a human owns it.
  10. Your top ~5 proven prompts (the prompt, what it's for, why it works); contribute your single best one to the team library each week.

Facilitator Guide

Prep checklist

  • Confirm every participant has completed and logged Module B (prerequisite).
  • Have the company AUP / data-classification rules on hand (the real ones, not the generic table).
  • Confirm which AI tools are sanctioned with verified training-opt-out — name them explicitly.
  • Set up a shared team prompt-library doc/sheet before the session; seed it with 2–3 examples.
  • Prepare role-relevant demo prompts for the Sales / Marketing / CS breakouts.
  • Brief managers on the 4-week nudge ritual (cross-reference Module G).

Materials needed: a sanctioned AI tool for live demo; the prompt card (Artifact A); the role library (Artifact B); the shared library doc; the AUP one-pager.

Timing guidance: the live session is 90 minutes; protect Part 3 (role breakouts) and Part 4 (review gate) — these are where Practitioner behavior forms. If you run short, compress Part 2, never Part 4.

Common pitfalls

  • Intimidation. The uncomfortable third disengages if the room skews to power users. Keep examples non-technical, celebrate small wins, and never let jargon creep in.
  • Skipping the gate. People love the speed and forget the review. Make Exercise 2 mandatory and have everyone share what they caught.
  • Tool confusion. If people aren't sure which tool is sanctioned, they'll either avoid AI or use a risky one. Name the tools explicitly and put them on a slide.
  • One-and-done. Without the four weeks of nudges, this reverts to Awareness. Get manager commitment before you start.

Discussion prompts

  • "Where in your week do you re-do the same kind of writing? That's your first prompt to save."
  • "What's one thing you'd never want to be wrong about in front of a customer? That's your review-gate line."
  • "Who on the team has a prompt the rest of us should steal?"

Tailoring by audience

  • Sales: lean on outreach drafting and call prep; emphasize "never invent facts about the prospect."
  • Marketing: lean on repurposing and variation; emphasize claim verification and brand voice.
  • CS: lean on response drafting and knowledge retrieval; emphasize confirming numbers (SLA, refunds, price) against the system of record.
  • Mixed non-technical (ops, admin, finance support): keep to the everyday-wins five (Part 2) and the rules (Part 5); the role breakout can be a general "your workflow" exercise.
