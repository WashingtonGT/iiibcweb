Shadow AI and safe tool use

Why this module exists

Shadow AI—the use of AI tools that IT, security, and legal never approved—is the dominant adoption pattern, not a fringe behavior. Roughly half to three-fifths of employees use unapproved AI tools (BlackFog and Cybernews surveys, 2025), while only about 23 percent use organization-provided ones (IDC, July 2025), and Cybersecurity Dive reports executives among the heaviest unsanctioned users. People are not being reckless; they are being productive with the easiest tool in front of them. The free chatbot is one tab away, and the sanctioned path, if one exists, is usually slower.

The danger is the data that travels with that convenience. An employee pastes a customer record, a snippet of proprietary source code, an unreleased financial model, or a patient's data into a consumer tool whose terms permit training on the input. The company has now handed confidential data to a third party it has no contract with, and it often cannot even discover that the leak happened. The breach-cost data is blunt: organizations with high shadow-AI exposure see materially higher average breach costs (IBM puts the premium around $670K over organizations with little or no shadow AI, 2025). Source code, legal documents, and M&A material are among the most commonly leaked categories.

Noise

"We'll just block all the AI tools." Bans don't work. They push usage onto personal devices and personal accounts where you have zero visibility, and a manageable governance problem becomes an invisible one.

Signal

The winning move is enablement, not prohibition. Give people a sanctioned tool that is at least as good and at least as easy, teach them the data rules, and give them an escalation path. Manage shadow AI by making the safe path the convenient one.

Note

Research note (directional): ~57 percent of AI users in some surveys actively hide their AI usage; ~38 percent admit sharing sensitive work data with AI without permission. The hiding is a symptom of fear-of-the-ban, not malice. Remove the fear and you regain visibility.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, participants will be able to:

  1. Define shadow AI and explain why it is the default pattern rather than the exception.
  2. Explain the three concrete harms of ungoverned tool use: data leakage to third parties, loss of visibility/auditability, and compliance exposure.
  3. Distinguish a sanctioned enterprise tool from a consumer tool and name what the enterprise tier buys (no-training-on-data, SSO, admin controls, retention settings, a signed DPA).
  4. Apply the data-classification rules to decide whether a given piece of information may go into any AI tool, and which tool.
  5. Use the company's approved-tool catalog and request a new tool through the proper process.
  6. Recognize indirect prompt-injection risk in untrusted content (pasting a webpage, email, or document into an AI tool).
  7. Escalate correctly when they encounter an unsanctioned tool, a suspected leak, or a use case the catalog doesn't cover.

Core content

Part 1: What shadow AI is, and why good people use it

Shadow AI is any use of an AI tool, model, or service that the organization has not vetted and approved for the data and task at hand. It comes in three shapes:

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  SHADOW AI — three common shapes                              │
  ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │  1. Unsanctioned tools   — free ChatGPT/Claude/Gemini on a    │
  │                            personal account, browser plugins   │
  │  2. Sanctioned tool,     — approved Copilot used on data it    │
  │     wrong data            was never cleared for                │
  │  3. Embedded AI features — "summarize with AI" buttons that    │
  │                            quietly ship data to a vendor       │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

People reach for shadow AI because it solves a real problem now. So the lesson is to stay productive on the sanctioned path, because that path protects you and the company.

Practitioner note

The most dangerous shadow AI is the kind that doesn't feel like AI: the "AI summarize" button in a note-taking app, the meeting transcriber that joins calls, the browser extension that "improves your writing." Each one is a data egress point. Treat any feature that sends your content somewhere to be "processed" as a tool that needs vetting.

Part 2: The three harms

Ungoverned tool use causes three distinct harms. Harm 1: Data leakage to an uncontracted third party. Consumer AI terms frequently permit the provider to retain and train on your inputs. Paste a customer list, source code, or an unreleased roadmap, and it may persist in a system you have no agreement with and cannot claw back. The canonical example is the engineering team that pasted proprietary source code into a public chatbot to debug it. The code left the building permanently.

Harm 2: Loss of visibility and auditability. If a tool isn't sanctioned, security can't log it, legal can't account for it in a data-subject request, and no one can answer "what AI touched this data?" after an incident. Invisible usage is unmanageable usage.

Harm 3: Compliance exposure. For regulated data (PII, PHI, financial, privileged), ungoverned AI use can breach GDPR, HIPAA, contractual data-handling terms, or sector rules in one paste. The EU AI Act's literacy and transparency obligations also assume you actually know which systems are in use (see Module L).

Risk flag

"It was just a quick question" is how most leaks start. The model doesn't forget the context you gave it to get a good answer. If the question contained classified data, the leak already happened. The answer quality is irrelevant.

Part 3: Consumer vs. enterprise tiers, and what you actually get

The tier you access a model through decides whether it is safe to use. This is the most important practical distinction in the module.

Signal

"Use the enterprise version" is not bureaucratic nagging. The enterprise tier is the difference between a model that helps you and a model that quietly keeps your customer's data. When in doubt, use the company-provisioned account, signed in with your company SSO.

Part 4: The decision: "Can I put this here?"

Combine the data-classification scheme from Module B with the tool tier. Every paste is a two-key decision: what is the data, and which tool am I using.

        WHAT IS THE DATA?
        ┌───────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────┐
        │ Public         │ Any sanctioned tool. Fine.            │
        │ Internal       │ Sanctioned enterprise tool only.      │
        │ Confidential   │ Sanctioned enterprise tool, no-train, │
        │ (customer,      │ + need-to-know. Check with owner.    │
        │  source, fin.)  │                                       │
        │ Restricted     │ Do NOT put in any AI tool without     │
        │ (PII/PHI/       │ explicit approval + DPA + legal sign- │
        │  privileged)    │ off. Default = no.                   │
        └───────────────┴───────────────────────────────────────┘

A second rule covers untrusted content you didn't write: pasting a random webpage, customer email, or third-party document into an AI tool can carry indirect prompt injection: hidden instructions in the content that hijack the model (covered in depth in Module J). Treat external content as untrusted input, not just data.

Part 5: Escalation and the approved-tool catalog

Every participant should leave knowing three things:

  1. Where the approved-tool catalog lives: the canonical list of sanctioned tools, the data tier each is cleared for, and how to access them.
  2. How to request a new tool: the lightweight intake (use case, data classification, vendor) that routes to security/IT for vetting. Fast approval is part of the deal: if requesting is slow, shadow AI wins.
  3. How to report a problem: an unsanctioned tool spreading on the team, a suspected leak ("I pasted something I shouldn't have"), or a use case the catalog doesn't cover. Reporting a mistake must be safe and blameless, because a hidden leak is far worse than a reported one.
Practitioner note

Pair this module with a real, published, short approved-tool catalog. Training people on rules with no sanctioned alternative just teaches them the company has no answer. It also confirms that shadow AI is the only way to get work done.

Hands-on lab and exercises

Lab K.1: "Can I put this here?" sort (15 min)

Present each item below. For each, participants decide: (a) the data classification, (b) whether it may go into an AI tool, and (c) which tool/tier.

#The scenario
1A blog post draft about industry trends, no customer names
2A customer's support ticket containing their email and account ID
3A snippet of our production authentication code, to debug it
4A list of prospects and deal sizes from the CRM
5A patient intake form (healthtech context)
6A public competitor's pricing page, pasted in to summarize
7The board deck for next week's meeting
8A meeting you want auto-transcribed by a new app you found

Facilitator answer key:

  1. Public → any sanctioned tool. Fine.
  2. Confidential/Restricted (contains PII) → sanctioned enterprise, no-train, need-to-know; strip identifiers if possible. Default caution.
  3. Confidential (proprietary + security-sensitive) → sanctioned enterprise no-train tool only; never a consumer tool. Auth code also warrants extra review (Module I).
  4. Confidential → sanctioned enterprise tool only; this is the company's competitive data.
  5. Restricted (PHI) → No without explicit approval, DPA, and legal sign-off. Default no.
  6. Public data, but it's untrusted external content: indirect-injection risk. Use a sanctioned tool and don't let it take actions based on the pasted content.
  7. Confidential/Restricted (material non-public) → sanctioned enterprise only, need-to-know.
  8. Stop: the transcriber is an unvetted tool and a data egress point. Route it through the approved-tool request process first.

Lab K.2: Find the shadow (team segment, 10 min)

Each participant lists, anonymously, every AI tool/feature they've used for work in the last month. Aggregate on a whiteboard. Mark each: ✓ sanctioned · ◑ sanctioned-but-wrong-data · ✗ shadow. This one exercise typically surfaces 3–10 tools leadership didn't know were in use. It also seeds the approved-tool catalog backlog.

Knowledge check

  1. (MCQ) Why are outright bans of AI tools usually counterproductive?
    a) They're illegal · b) They push usage to personal devices/accounts where there's no visibility · c) They slow down the model · d) They violate the EU AI Act
  2. (MCQ) What does an enterprise/managed AI tier typically provide that a consumer free tier does not? (choose all): SSO · no-training-on-data · admin logs · a signed DPA · faster model
  3. (T/F) It's fine to paste a customer's PII into any AI tool as long as you delete the chat afterward.
  4. (Short answer) Name the two "keys" in the can-I-put-this-here decision.
  5. (MCQ) You want to use a new AI meeting-transcriber you found. What's the right first step?
    a) Use it for one meeting to test it · b) Submit it through the approved-tool request process · c) Use it only for internal meetings · d) Ask a colleague if they've used it
  6. (Short answer) What risk arises specifically from pasting untrusted external content (a webpage or email) into an AI tool?
  7. (Short answer) Why is a blameless reporting path essential to managing shadow AI?
  8. (MCQ) Which is the most dangerous because it doesn't feel like "AI use"? a) ChatGPT in a browser · b) An "AI summarize" button embedded in an app · c) Copilot in the IDE · d) A sanctioned enterprise chatbot

Answer key

1-b · 2: SSO, no-training, admin logs, signed DPA (not "faster model") · 3-False (deleting the chat doesn't undo retention/training; restricted data needs approval + DPA) · 4: what is the data (classification) and which tool/tier · 5-b · 6: indirect prompt injection: hidden instructions in the content can hijack the model · 7: because fear of punishment makes people hide leaks; a hidden leak is far worse, and far harder to manage, than a reported one · 8-b.

Facilitator guide

Prep checklist:

  • Have a real, current approved-tool catalog and request link ready. This module fails without a sanctioned alternative to point to.
  • Confirm the enterprise tiers you reference are actually provisioned (SSO, no-train) so your claims are true.
  • Pull 2–3 anonymized real examples of shadow AI from your own org (from a discovery scan or survey). Local examples land harder than statistics.

Timing: Keep it to 45–60 min. This is an Awareness-level module, so resist going deep into injection mechanics (that's Module J).

Common pitfalls:

  • Delivering rules with no sanctioned path → teaches helplessness, entrenches shadow AI.
  • Shaming people for past shadow use → guarantees future hiding. Lead with "this is normal; here's the safe way."
  • Treating embedded "AI features" as out of scope → they're the biggest blind spot.

Discussion prompts:

  • "What's the AI tool you reach for when the sanctioned one is too slow—and what would make the sanctioned one win?"
  • "What's a piece of data you've been unsure whether you could put into an AI tool?"

Tailoring by audience: For managers and IT/security, add a 20-min appendix on discovery (SSO/IdP log analysis, prompt-level DLP, SASE integration) and on keeping approval turnaround fast. For GTM and ops, weight Lab K.1 toward customer-data and CRM scenarios.
